Latin Suffixes

-tion / -sion / -cion (action / state / result)

The big idea
The most common suffix in English — from Latin -tionem. Three spellings, one /shun/ sound, thousands of words. The right spelling depends on the root that precedes it.
The pro move
EXACERBATION
Say it. ih-gza-ser-BAY-shuhn Spell it. E · X · A · C · E · R · B · A · T · I · O · N Say it again. ih-gza-ser-BAY-shuhn Syllables. ih · gzae · ser · bey · shah · n Sounds → Letters. [eg=EX] [ZAS=AC] [er=ER] [BAY=BA] [shun=TION] Exception. -tion = /shun/ always; from exacerbare = make harsh; ex- prefix; stress: eg-ZAS-er-BAY-shun Origin. Latin Root. Latin acerbus = harsh, bitter

The Three Spellings

-tion = after most roots: exacerbation, perdition, scintillation. -sion = after d, l, n, r + vowel: extension, mansion, revision. -ssion = after stressed vowel: mission, passion, session, permission. -cion = rare: coercion, suspicion.

The Decision Rule

Does the root end in d or se? Use -sion: extend → extension, revise → revision. Does it end in t or te? Use -tion: act → action, create → creation. Does it end in ess? Use -ssion: express → expression, suppress → suppression. The root ending is your guide.

-tion Spelling Patterns

Before -tion, a c sometimes appears: infect → infection, connect → connection. Before -tion, a p sometimes appears: prescribe → prescription (-ption). Assume → assumption (-ption). These consonant mutations come from how Latin combined roots with the suffix.

High-Value Championship Words

Propitiation = winning favor through a ritual act. Expiation = atoning for a wrong. Supplication = humble begging. Predilection (pre- + dilectus = chosen) = a strong preference. Juxtaposition (juxta = beside + posit) = placement side by side. All end in -tion.

Counting Syllables in -tion Words

-tion is always ONE syllable = /shun/. Count carefully for long words: exacerbation = 5 syllables (ex·ac·er·ba·tion). Predestination = 6. Transubstantiation = 7. The Bizzing Bee Sound Trick (syllable by syllable) is essential for these long words.

Latin Suffixes

-ous / -ious / -eous / -uous (full of / having quality)

The big idea
The -ous adjective family means "full of" or "having the quality of." Choosing -ious, -eous, or -uous depends on the noun base. These endings produce some of the richest vocabulary in English.
The pro move
PERSPICACIOUS
Say it. per-spuh-KAY-shuhs Spell it. P · E · R · S · P · I · C · A · C · I · O · U · S Say it again. per-spuh-KAY-shuhs Syllables. per · spi · ca · cious Sounds → Letters. [per=PER] [spih=SPI] [KAY=CA] [shus=CIOUS] Exception. -acious from Latin -ax stem; noun form = perspicACITY (same -cac- spelling, different stress) Origin. Latin Root. Latin perspicere = to see clearly, from per = through + specere = to l

The Four Forms

-ous: enormous, famous. -ious (abstract nouns in -y or -ce): audacious, facetious. -eous (inherent quality): simultaneous, extraneous, igneous. -uous (manner or habit): assiduous, ambiguous, sensuous. Each variant has a pattern.

The -ious Treasure Words — the -acious Cluster

From the Latin -ax stem: perspicacious (sharply insightful), sagacious (wise), tenacious (holding on), voracious (devouring), pugnacious (fighting), loquacious (talkative), audacious (bold), vivacious (lively). All turn into -acity nouns: perspicacity, sagacity, tenacity.

-eous Words

Miscellaneous = of mixed kinds. Extraneous = from outside. Simultaneous = at the same time. Spontaneous = arising on its own. Igneous (ignis = fire) = formed from volcanic fire. Erroneous = containing error. Nauseous = causing nausea. These use -eous because the root has an -an- or bare -e- base.

-uous Words

Describe habitual manner: Assiduous = habitually diligent. Ambiguous = two-way. Conspicuous = clearly visible. Tenuous = very thin/weak. Impetuous = acting without thinking. Perspicuous = clearly and plainly expressed. Promiscuous = indiscriminately mixed. The -u- is part of the Latin stem.

Spelling Traps

Facetious = F-A-C-E-T-I-O-U-S — contains all five vowels A-E-I-O-U in order! A fun mnemonic. Mischievous = MIS-CHIEV-OUS (3 syllables, NOT 4). Grievous = GRIEV-OUS (not griev-i-ous). Adding an extra -i- to these is the most common error with this suffix family.

Latin Suffixes

-able / -ible (capable of being)

The big idea
Two spellings, one /uh-bul/ sound. The right choice depends on whether the root can stand alone as an English word. Championship spellers know both the rule and the exceptions.
The pro move
INDELIBLE
Say it. ih-NDEH-lih-buhl Spell it. I · N · D · E · L · I · B · L · E Say it again. ih-NDEH-lih-buhl Syllables. ih · ndeh · lih · bah · l Sounds → Letters. [in=IN] [DEL=DEL] [uh=I] [bul=BLE] Exception. -ible not -able because root dele- cannot stand alone as an English word; in- = not; stress: in-DEL-uh-bul Origin. Latin Root. Latin indelebilis = indestructible, from in+delere = to destroy/erase

The Main Rule

Use -able when the root is a complete English word: comfort → comfortable, depend → dependable, break → breakable. Use -ible when the root is a Latin stem that cannot stand alone: aud- → audible, leg- → legible, vis- → visible, del- → indelible. Root completeness is the test.

The -ible List (Memorize These 20)

contemptible, digestible, discernible, divisible, eligible, fallible, feasible, flexible, gullible, indelible, intelligible, irresistible, legible, permissible, plausible, sensible, susceptible, tangible, terrible, visible. When you cannot identify the root as a standalone English word, try -ible.

Negated -able/-ible

in- + -able: incomprehensible, inscrutable, inexorable, inimitable, intractable. in- + -ible: indelible, infallible, ineligible, inflexible, irresistible. Knowing the negative prefix tells you the base suffix — and the base suffix tells you the spelling.

-able After -ce and -ge

Keep the e before -able to preserve the soft sound: notice → noticeable, change → changeable, manage → manageable, knowledge → knowledgeable. Without the e, the c or g would harden. The e is the softening guard.

Championship -ible Traps

Irresistible = I-R-R-E-S-I-S-T-I-B-L-E (double-r from ir- assimilation). Contemptible (capable of being contempted) vs contemptuous (showing contempt) — different suffix, different meaning. Indefatigable (in- + de- + fatigare = tire) = untiring — uses -able despite the complex root.

Latin Suffixes

-ance / -ence / -ancy / -ency (state / quality)

The big idea
These noun suffixes create abstract nouns from adjectives. The rule: adjectives in -ant become -ance; adjectives in -ent become -ence. But French-origin words may not follow — and those are the ones in bees.
The pro move
EFFERVESCENCE
Say it. ef-er-VES-ents Spell it. E · F · F · E · R · V · E · S · C · E · N · C · E Say it again. ef-er-VES-ents Syllables. ef · fer · ves · cence Sounds → Letters. [ef=EF] [fer=FER] [VES=VES] [ents=CENCE] Exception. -escence = becoming (Latin -escere); -sc- cluster is the hard part; double-f from ef- assimilation Origin. French Root. French: effervescence (French)

The Core Rule

Adjective ending -ant → noun ending -ance: relevant → relevance, important → importance, defiant → defiance. Adjective ending -ent → noun ending -ence: different → difference, evident → evidence, prominent → prominence. The adjective tells you the noun.

High-Value -ence Words

Effervescence = bubbly, sparkling quality. Eloquence = beautiful speaking. Benevolence = goodwill. Magniloquence = pompous speech. Renaissance (re- + naissance = birth) = R-E-N-A-I-S-S-A-N-C-E — double-s! Obeisance (-ance not -ence!) = respectful bow. Prescience = foreknowledge.

-escent = Becoming

-escent = in the process of becoming. Effervescent = becoming bubbly. Adolescent = becoming an adult. Luminescent = becoming light-emitting. Convalescent = becoming well again. Incandescent = becoming glowingly hot. Quiescent = becoming quiet/still. The -sc- cluster in all of these is the spelling challenge.

French Exceptions

French-origin words may not follow the -ant/-ent rule. Nuance = -ance (from French, no -ant adjective). Vengeance = -ance (not -ence). Conscience = -ence (from con- + scire). For words you cannot analyze, asking for the language of origin at the microphone helps — French words are more likely to break the rule.

-ancy/-ency (Dynamic Forms)

These match their base adjectives: buoyant → buoyancy, truant → truancy, vacant → vacancy (all -ancy). Decent → decency, efficient → efficiency, urgent → urgency (all -ency). The dynamic forms -ancy/-ency are simply the -ance/-ence forms with the suffix -y attached to a slightly shortened base.

Latin & Old English Suffixes

-ity / -ness / -hood / -ship / -dom (abstract state)

The big idea
Five suffixes that convert other words into abstract nouns — from different linguistic origins, covering different territory. Together they create the vocabulary of qualities, conditions, and realms.
The pro move
PERSPICACITY
Say it. — Spell it. P · E · R · S · P · I · C · A · C · I · T · Y Say it again. — Syllables. p · e · r · s · p · i · c · a · c · i · t · y Sounds → Letters. [per=PER] [spuh=SPI] [KAS=CAC] [uh=I] [tee=TY] Exception. noun form of perspicacious; -acity from -acious adjectives; -cac- not -cak-; stress: per-spuh-KAS-uh-tee Origin. — Root. —

-ity (Latin)

The most formal abstract suffix from Latin -itas. Perspicacity, sagacity, audacity, mendacity, loquacity, voracity, tenacity, vivacity — all from -acious adjectives. Also: brevity (brevis = short), levity (levis = light), gravity (gravis = heavy), probity (probus = honest).

-ness (Old English)

The most flexible abstract suffix — works with almost any adjective. Happiness, sadness, darkness (everyday). Also with complex Latinate adjectives: conscientiousness, fastidiousness, scrupulousness. When no -ity form exists, -ness is always available. That is why both weirdness and (no weirdity) work.

-hood (Old English)

Limited to specific life stages or communities: childhood, adulthood, knighthood, priesthood, neighborhood, falsehood (abstract!). Cannot be added freely — only to established words. The -hood suffix always implies membership in a state or condition.

-ship (Old English)

Scholarship, sportsmanship, workmanship, friendship, hardship, stewardship, ownership. Also: kinship, fellowship, penmanship. The suffix carries a sense of skill, relationship, or status. Seamanship = the skill of a seaman — both the role and the craft.

-dom (Old English)

Kingdom = realm of a king. Freedom = state of being free. Wisdom = state of being wise. Martyrdom = state of being a martyr. Used ironically for large groups: officialdom, boredom, stardom. Still actively used to coin new words in contemporary English — fandom is a modern -dom coinage.

Agent Suffixes: -er / -or / -ist / -ian / -eur / -eer

The big idea
When English says "a person who does something," it borrows from six different languages. Origin determines suffix. The French -eur and -eer words are the ones that trip spellers most.
The pro move
RESTAURATEUR
Say it. reh-ster-uh-TUR Spell it. R · E · S · T · A · U · R · A · T · E · U · R Say it again. reh-ster-uh-TUR Syllables. reh · ster · ah · ter Sounds → Letters. [res=RES] [tor=TOR] [uh=A] [TUR=TEUR] Exception. French restaurer (restore) + -eur; NO -n- before -eur; restauranteur is WRONG; stress: res-tor-uh-TUR Origin. French Root. French restaurer = to restore + -ateur = one who does

-or (Latin) vs -er (Old English/Germanic)

Latin roots take -or: actor, senator, elevator, narrator. Old English/Germanic roots take -er: builder, teacher, baker, singer. Works about 85% of the time. For a Latinate word you are unsure about: try -or first. Governor not governour.

-ist (Greek) = Practitioner or Believer

Added to nouns: pianist, violinist, cyclist. Added to ideologies: socialist, nihilist. Added to Greek roots: botanist, physicist. Lyricist = one who writes lyrics. Illusionist = one who creates illusions. The -ist ending always signals a person with a specific expertise or belief.

-ian / -an (Latin) = One Who Practices

Words ending in -ic drop the c before -ian: magic → magician, electric → electrician, music → musician, logic → logician. Words ending in -ist sometimes become -ian for specialist titles: pediatrician (not pediatrist). Mathematician = M-A-T-H-E-M-A-T-I-C-I-A-N.

-eur (French) = One Who Does (Formal)

Always -eur not -er for these French-origin agent words: connoisseur, chauffeur, raconteur, saboteur, voyageur, entrepreneur, provocateur. ⚠️ Restaurateur (one who restores/runs a restaurant) = NOT restauranteur. One of the most common misspellings in educated English.

-eer (Mixed) = One Who Works With

Old French origin, now naturalized: auctioneer, engineer (ingen- = clever device + -eer), pioneer, volunteer, puppeteer, profiteer. Also -ier (French variant): barrier, rapier, financier, cavalier. The -eer/-ier ending usually carries the stress: auction-EER, financ-IER.

The big idea
Rhetorical vocabulary describes the techniques of persuasion, deception, and argument. From Aristotle through modern debate, these terms appear in championship bees and are built from Greek and Latin roots.
The pro move

Deception Techniques

Prevarication (pre- + varicare = straddle) = evasive speech. Equivocation (equi- + vox = equal voice) = deliberately ambiguous language. Dissimulation (dis- + simulare = pretend) = concealing true feelings. Obfuscation (ob- + fuscare = to darken) = deliberately making things unclear. Dissembling = disguising true motives.

Rhetorical Figures — Saying by Not Saying

Apophasis (apo- = away + phasis = speech) = mentioning something by saying you will not mention it ("I won't mention his criminal record..."). Litotes (litos = plain) = understatement by negation ("not bad" = very good). Meiosis (meioun = to lessen) = deliberate understatement.

Figures of Emphasis

Anaphora (ana- = back + phora = carrying) = repetition at the start of successive clauses. Epistrophe (epi- + strephein = turn) = repetition at the end of clauses. Chiasmus (khiasmos = diagonal arrangement) = ABBA inversion: "Ask not what your country can do for you; ask what you can do for your country." Zeugma (zeugma = yoke) = one verb governing two different objects.

Logical Fallacies

Sophistry = clever but misleading argument. Solecism (Soloi = a Greek colony with bad Greek) = a grammatical error or social blunder. Malapropism = using the wrong word for a similar-sounding one. Pleonasm (pleon = more) = using more words than necessary ("free gift," "ATM machine"). Tautology (tauto = same) = needlessly repeating the same idea.

High-Value Bee Rhetorical Words

Apophasis = A-P-O-P-H-A-S-I-S (-ph- Greek signal). Chiasmus = C-H-I-A-S-M-U-S (-ch- = /k/). Zeugma = Z-E-U-G-M-A (-eu- diphthong). Tmesis (tmesis = cutting) = inserting a word inside another word ("what-SO-ever") = T-M-E-S-I-S (starts with tm- — unusual cluster). Anacoluthon = a sentence that starts one way and ends another.

Advanced Spelling Strategy

The Schwa Problem & Unstressed Vowels

The big idea
The schwa — the unstressed /uh/ sound — is the most common vowel sound in English and can be spelled with any of the five vowels. Solving the schwa is the single biggest challenge in advanced spelling.
The pro move
SEPARATE
Say it. SEH-per-ayt Spell it. S · E · P · A · R · A · T · E Say it again. SEH-per-ayt Syllables. seh · per · ey · t Sounds → Letters. [SEP=SEP] [ur=AR] [ut=ATE] Exception. middle syllable = /ur/ schwa spelled -ar-; common error is seperate with -er-; stress: SEP-ur-ut Origin. Latin Root. Latin separare = to pull apart (se=apart + parare=prepare/arrange)

What is the Schwa? The schwa (ə) is the neutral, unstressed /uh/ sound. It can be spelled: a (about), e (the), i (pencil), o (lemon), u (circus). Separate* has a schwa in the middle: sep-ar-ate — the ar is reduced to /ur/. Common error: seperate (using e instead of a*). 🃏 Card 2 — **Strategy 1 — Stress the Related Word

Find a related word where the unstressed syllable BECOMES stressed: Competition → stress the compete → /com-PETE/ shows the e in pet. Explanation → explain → /ex-PLAIN/ shows the a. Definition → define → /de-FINE/ shows the i. The stressed form reveals the correct vowel.

Strategy 2 — Know the Suffix

Suffix patterns are reliable: -ant words use -ance: important → importance. -ent words use -ence: evident → evidence. -ary vs -ery: secretary (Latin origin → -ary) vs stationery (from stationer → -ery). The suffix rule beats guessing the schwa sound.

Strategy 3 — Know the Origin

Latin words use -or not -er for agents: senator, elevator, narrator. French words use -eur not -er: entrepreneur, chauffeur. Greek words use -is not -us for some nouns: thesis, crisis, basis (but plural -es: theses, crises). Origin predicts the unstressed vowel.

The Most-Misspelled Schwa Words

Separate (-ar-), definitely (-ite- not -ate-), environment (-ron-), government (-rn-), February (-ru-), library (-ra-), necessary (-ess-), privilege (-leg-), restaurant (-au-). Each has a schwa that is a misspelling trap. The strategy: stress the root to reveal the vowel, or memorize the origin.

The 44 Phonemes — Sound-to-Spelling Mastery

The big idea
English has 26 letters but 44 distinct sounds (phonemes). The Bizzing Bee Sound Trick works by mapping sounds to letters. This concept covers the hardest sound-spelling mismatches — the ones where one sound has multiple possible spellings.
The pro move
ONOMATOPOEIA
Say it. on-oh-mat-oh-PEE-uh Spell it. O · N · O · M · A · T · O · P · O · E · I · A Say it again. on-oh-mat-oh-PEE-uh Syllables. on · o · mat · o · poe · ia Sounds → Letters. [on=ON] [oh=O] [mat=MAT] [oh=O] [PEE=POE] [uh=IA] Exception. Greek onoma = name + poiein = make = naming by sound; -eia = Greek feminine ending; 6 syllables Origin. Greek Root. The /f/ Sound — 4 Spellings

f = fun, fish (everyday words). ph = phone, graph (Greek words). ff = off, cliff, staff (after short vowel at end). gh = enough, rough, tough (Old English). Rule: single everyday word → f; Greek origin → ph; after short vowel at end → ff; Old English + vowel before → gh. Origin + position = correct spelling.

The /k/ Sound — 5 Spellings

k = king, kind (before e/i). c = cat, cup, cold (before a/o/u). ck = lack, brick, deck (after short vowel at end of syllable). ch = chemistry, echo (Greek). que = unique, clique (French). Rule: before e/i → k; before a/o/u → c; after short vowel → ck; Greek → ch; French end → que. Origin and position decide.

The /sh/ Sound — 7 Spellings

sh = ship, wish (Old English). ti = nation, patience (Latin -tio). ci = special, musician (Latin). ssi = mission, session (Latin). ch = champagne, chef (French). s = sugar, sure. xi = anxious. ⚠️ Most common: -tion, -sion, -cian suffixes all produce /sh/ from the ti/ci/ssi patterns.

The Schwa /uh/ — Any Vowel

The schwa can be spelled a (about), e (the), i (pencil), o (lemon), u (circus), ai (certain). The only solution: stress the related word to reveal the hidden vowel, know the suffix rules, or know the etymology. No shortcut exists — but patterns narrow the options dramatically.

Onomatopoeia — Words That Sound Like What They Mean

Onomatopoeia (onoma + poiein = make a name) = naming by imitation of sound: buzz, hiss, crash, murmur, babble, cackle, sizzle, thud. These are words where the sound IS the meaning. Onomatopoeia itself = O-N-O-M-A-T-O-P-O-E-I-A — one of the longest words with multiple repeated vowel pairs.

Personality: Generosity & Miserliness

The big idea
From magnanimous givers to parsimonious hoarders, this cluster of words describes attitudes toward sharing, spending, and keeping. Built primarily on Latin roots of giving and withholding.
The pro move
MUNIFICENT
Say it. myoo-NIF-ih-sent Spell it. M · U · N · I · F · I · C · E · N · T Say it again. myoo-NIF-ih-sent Syllables. m · u · n · i · f · i · c · e · n · t Sounds → Letters. Latin munificus = generous, from munus=gift + facere=make

Generous Words

Munificent (munus = gift + facere = make) = extremely generous. Magnanimous (magna = great + animus = soul) = great-souled generosity. Beneficent (bene + facere) = actively doing good. Bountiful = providing generously. Openhanded = giving freely. Philanthropic = motivated by love of humanity.

Miserly Words

Parsimonious (parsimonia = sparingness) = excessively frugal. Penurious (penuria = want) = extremely poor OR excessively mean with money. Niggardly (Old Norse hngurr = miser) = miserly. Avaricious (avarus = greedy) = extremely greedy for wealth. Rapacious (rapax = seizing) = greedily seizing more than needed.

The munificent-parsimony Scale

From most to least generous: munificent → magnanimous → beneficent → generous → thrifty → frugal → parsimonious → miserly → avaricious → rapacious. Knowing these distinctions wins vocabulary rounds.

Spending Attitudes

Prodigal (prodigere = drive forward wastefully) = recklessly extravagant. Profligate (profligare = strike down) = wildly wasteful. Spendthrift = spending recklessly. Lavish = spending in large amounts. These describe too much generosity, not virtue.

Spelling These Words

Munificent = M-U-N-I-F-I-C-E-N-T. Parsimonious = P-A-R-S-I-M-O-N-I-O-U-S. Avaricious = A-V-A-R-I-C-I-O-U-S (-cious suffix). Rapacious = R-A-P-A-C-I-O-U-S. Penurious = P-E-N-U-R-I-O-U-S. All end in -ious — the pattern from -acious/-icious family.

The big idea
Championship rounds include words from Hebrew, Malay, Swahili, Welsh, and other less-common source languages. These have distinctive patterns that cannot be decoded from Latin or Greek knowledge alone.

Hebrew Loanwords

Jubilee (yobhel = ram's horn = the year of the horn blast = a 50-year celebration). Amen = so be it. Cherub (kerubh) = a type of angel. Leviathan (liwyathan = sea monster). Sabbath (shabbat = rest). Messiah (mashiach = anointed one). Hebrew words often have doubled letters from the original consonantal script.

Malay Loanwords

Amok (amuk = furious attack) = "running amok." Orangutan (orang = person + hutan = forest) = "person of the forest" — O-R-A-N-G-U-T-A-N. Bamboo (mambu). Rattan (rotan). Gong (gong). Malay loanwords came to English through trade in Southeast Asia — they follow CV syllable patterns similar to Japanese.

Welsh Loanwords

Corgi (cor = dwarf + gi = dog) = dwarf dog. Flannel (Welsh gwlanen = wool). Penguin (possibly Welsh pen gwyn = white head). Cwtch (a Welsh hiding place or cuddle). Welsh has unusual consonant patterns — ll = a lateral fricative, ch = /kh/. In English, these are simplified.

Afrikaans/Dutch Loanwords

Aardvark (aarde = earth + vark = pig) = earth pig. Apartheid (apart = separate + -heid = -hood = separateness). Wildebeest (wilde = wild + beest = beast). Springbok (spring = jump + bok = deer). These keep Dutch/Afrikaans spelling and can be decoded by recognizing their compound structure.

Bee Strategy for Rare Languages

When nothing from Latin, Greek, French, or German applies — ask for language of origin AND a definition AND a sentence. The combination of all three clues usually reveals the pattern. For truly unknown languages: try to spell what you hear phonetically. Many rare-language loanwords are more phonetic than Latin or Greek words.
