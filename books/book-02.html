These initial consonant clusters come from Old English and Norse. The first consonant was once pronounced — you can still hear it in German (Knabe = boy, schreiben = to write). English pronunciation shifted; spelling froze.
The pro move

WR- (silent W)

wr- cluster from Old English: wraith (ghost), writhe (twist in pain), wrought (past of work), wrath (fury), wreak (inflict), wrest (twist away), wry (twisted/ironic), wring (twist tightly), wren (small bird). All start with a silent w — only the r is heard.

KN- (silent K)

Old Norse kn- cluster: knight, knave, kneel, knell (sound of a bell), knapsack, knack, knavish, knickers, knob, knoll (small hill), knot. The k was once pronounced — you can still hear it in German Knabe (boy) and Knie (knee).

GN- (silent G)

Gnome (small creature), gnarled (twisted), gnash (grind teeth), gnu (wildebeest), gnostic (gnosis = knowledge — the g was always silent in Greek), gnaw (chew persistently), gnarl (twisted lump). The g was once pronounced in Old English — you can still hear it in Italian gnocchi.

Chapter 2 · Silent -gh- PatternsThe idea · ★★☆
The big idea
The -gh- cluster is Old English's most complex legacy. It was once pronounced as a guttural /kh/ sound (like German Bach). As that sound disappeared, the gh became silent — but stayed in the spelling, creating six different pronunciations for -ough alone.
The pro move
DISTRAUGHT
Say it. d-ih-s-t-r-AW-t Spell it. D · I · S · T · R · A · U · G · H · T Say it again. d-ih-s-t-r-AW-t Syllables. d · i · s · t · r · a · u · g · h · t Sounds → Letters. [dis=DIS] [TRAWT=TRAUGHT] Exception. -aught = /awt/ sound; gh is completely silent; from Old English trahtion = pulled; stress: dis-TRAWT Origin. English Root. Latin distractus = pulled apart, past participle of distrahere

-igh- = /eye/ sound

Night, knight, blight, flight, right, sight, tight, light, might, slight, fright, plight, bright. The -igh- sequence always = /eye/. The gh is completely silent. This is the most regular of the -gh- patterns.

-ough = 6 pronunciations (memorize as groups)

- /oh/: though, although, dough, furlough - /oo/: through, slough (a swamp) - /uf/: rough, tough, enough, slough (shed skin) - /of/: cough, trough - /aw/: thought, bought, fought, ought, nought, distraught, wrought - /up/: hiccough (= hiccup) Learn by pronunciation group, not individual words.

-aught = /awt/ sound

Distraught (pulled apart = extremely upset), onslaught (sudden violent attack), slaughter, naught (nothing), naughty, daughter, caught, taught, fraught (filled with anxiety). All use -aught = /awt/. This group is the most consistent.

-ight vs -ite

Night (Old English, -ight) vs site (Latin, -ite). Might (power) vs mite (a tiny creature). Right vs write vs rite — three spellings of one /rīt/ sound. When the /eye/ + /t/ sound appears in a word with Old English feel, the spelling is likely -ight.

Chapter 3 · ie / ei Rule and ExceptionsThe idea · ★☆☆
The big idea
The classic rhyme: "i before e, except after c, or when sounding like /ay/ as in neighbor and weigh." This covers 80% of cases. The other 20% — the exceptions — are the ones that appear in spelling bees.
The pro move
SERENDIPITY
Say it. s-eh-r-uh-n-d-IH-p-ih-t-ee Spell it. S · E · R · E · N · D · I · P · I · T · Y Say it again. s-eh-r-uh-n-d-IH-p-ih-t-ee Syllables. s · e · r · e · n · d · i · p · i · t · y Sounds → Letters. [ser=SER] [en=EN] [DIP=DIP] [ih=I] [tee=TY] Exception. -i- before -p- here; no ie/ei issue in this word — but seize, weird, leisure all break the ie/ei rule Origin. French Root. Persian Serendip = old name for Sri Lanka

The Rule — i before e

After most consonants, ie = /ee/: believe, achieve, relieve, reprieve, grieve, retrieve, mischief, piece, niece. The mnemonic: "i before e" — the i comes first in most /ee/ sounds.

After c — e before i

After the letter c, the order reverses: ceiling, receive, perceive, deceive, conceive, deceit, conceit, receipt. The c flips the order. Mnemonic: the c is selfish — it pushes e in front of i.

When Sounding /ay/ — e before i

Vein, reign, rein, sleigh, weigh, beige, feign, feint, freight, neighbor, eight. When the vowel combination sounds like /ay/, e comes before i. The sound /ay/ overrides the normal order.

The Exceptions — Memorize These

Seize (not sieze), weird (not wierd), either (not iether), neither, leisure (not liesure), forfeit (not forfiet), foreign (not foriegn), height (not hieght), protein, caffeine, kaleidoscope, zeitgeist, species. These break the rule and must be memorized individually.

Chapter 4 · Double Consonant RulesThe idea · ★★☆
The big idea
English doubles a final consonant before suffixes that begin with a vowel (-ing, -ed, -er, -est) under specific conditions. The rule is completely systematic — stress is the key.
The pro move
COMMITTED
Say it. kuh-MIH-tihd Spell it. C · O · M · M · I · T · T · E · D Say it again. kuh-MIH-tihd Syllables. kah · mih · tah · d Sounds → Letters. [kuh=COM] [MIT=MIT] [ed=TED] Exception. stress on -MIT so double T before -ed; commit + ted → committed; single-T would give /KOM-ih-ted/ Origin. Latin Root. Latin committere = to send or put together

The One-Syllable Rule (CVC)

Double the final consonant when a one-syllable word ends in consonant-vowel-consonant: run→running, stop→stopped, sit→sitting, cut→cutting, big→bigger. The doubling keeps the vowel short. Without it: runing would rhyme with tuning.

The Two-Syllable Stress Rule

For two-syllable words, double only if stress falls on the LAST syllable: begin→beginning (stress on BEGIN), occur→occurred (stress on OCCUR), commit→committed (stress on COMMIT), prefer→preferred (stress on preFER). Do NOT double if stress falls on the FIRST syllable: open→opening, happen→happened.

Test — Tap the Syllable

Tap your desk on each syllable and feel which one is louder. Travel: TRAV-el (first syllable louder) → traveled (no double). Control: con-TROL (second syllable louder) → controlled (double-l). Offer: OF-fer (first) → offered (no double). Occur: oc-CUR (second) → occurred (double-r).

American vs British Doubling

Americans: traveled, modeled, canceled. British: travelled, modelled, cancelled — they double whenever the final l is preceded by a single vowel, regardless of stress. For US spelling bees (Merriam-Webster): American conventions apply. Know which dictionary the bee uses.

The big idea
The letter c has two sounds: /k/ (hard) before a, o, u, and /s/ (soft) before e, i, y. Knowing this rule explains dozens of spelling patterns and helps decode words you've never seen.

The big idea

The big idea
Homophones are words that sound identical but are spelled differently and mean different things. They cannot be decoded from sound alone — etymology and context are the only guides.
The pro move
PRINCIPLE
Say it. PRIH-nsuh-puhl Spell it. P · R · I · N · C · I · P · L · E Say it again. PRIH-nsuh-puhl Syllables. prih · nsah · pah · l Sounds → Letters. The Core Pairs

Principal (the head of a school, or primary) vs Principle (a fundamental rule). Memory trick: the principal is your pal; a principle is a learned rule. Complement (complete something) vs Compliment (praise). Stationary (not moving) vs Stationery (paper products — the er in stationery = paper).

More Critical Pairs

Affect (verb = to influence) vs Effect (noun = the result). Accept (to receive) vs Except (excluding). Elicit (to draw out) vs Illicit (not permitted). Eminent (famous, prominent) vs Imminent (about to happen). Allude (hint at) vs Elude (escape from).

Triple Homophones

Their (belonging to them) / There (a place) / They're (they are). To (preposition) / Two (the number) / Too (also/excessively). Bare (uncovered) / Bear (the animal, or to carry). Three spellings, one sound — context is everything.

Etymology Resolves Homophones

Stationary comes from Latin stationarius = standing still. Stationery comes from stationer = a bookshop owner who had a fixed station (shop). The -er at the end of stationery comes from the stationer who sold paper. Knowing the origin makes the spelling logical.
