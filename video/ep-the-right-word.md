# The Right Word

**Runtime target:** ~8:00 · **Voice:** the narrator from episode one. **One guest voice.**

---

## WHAT THIS FILM IS ABOUT

> **Somebody reaches for a word, and afterwards the world is different.**

That is the whole message. There is not a second one.

Four true stories, all proving that same single thing, arranged so each is bigger than the
last:

| | Story | What the word changed |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Čapek and *robot*, 1920 | **a thing** — a word that didn't exist, now in almost every language |
| 2 | Hamilton's hurricane letter, 1772 | **a life** — a penniless boy crosses an ocean |
| 3 | Nehru at midnight, 1947 | **a country** — *tryst*, not *promise* |
| 4 | Lincoln at Gettysburg, 1863 | **what a war meant** — 272 words |

The close connects it to spelling in four sentences and stops. Nothing else.

### What was cut, and why

The last three drafts failed the same way: too many messages. This removes them rather than
rearranging them.

- **Frederick Douglass is gone.** His story is about being *denied* words — access, not
  choice — so it proves a different point, and it is far too big to be an on-ramp. Using a
  child who wished himself an animal as the set-up for "and that's what a spelling bee is
  for" was the tonal collision that made every draft feel wrong. **He deserves his own film,
  with no app pitch anywhere in it.**
- **The English-is-three-languages act is gone.** Genuinely fun, and a completely separate
  subject. It is its own episode: *Why Is English Spelled Like That?*
- **Stephen King, Stan Lee, Shakespeare, Everett's letter, Mahalia Jackson, Lemkin** — all
  gone. Each was proving its own small point.

### One guest voice

**NEHRU**, and only Nehru — an Indian voice for the moment India speaks. Everything else is
the narrator. `prep2.py` reads speakers off the `**SPEAKER:**` tags, so adding one back is one

## THE FACTS (check before recording)

**Robot.** Karel Čapek's play *R.U.R.*, published **1920**, premiered Prague Jan 1921. He
publicly credited his brother **Josef** with the word, from Czech ***robota*** — the forced
labour a serf owed his lord. Čapek's own account: he first tried *labori* and hated it.
⚠️ In the TTS text spell it **Robota**, with **no phonetic hint in the direction** — two
spellings of one word produced neither.

**Hamilton.** Hurricane struck **St Croix, 31 Aug 1772**. Hamilton, about 17, wrote an account
of it; published in the *Royal Danish-American Gazette* **3 Oct 1772**; local subscribers
raised funds to send him to North America. ⚠️ Scholarship differs on the mechanics — the
letter was **part of** what got him off the island, not provably the sole cause. He was
orphaned/abandoned and working as a clerk; say "about seventeen."

**Nehru, 14 Aug 1947**, Constituent Assembly, New Delhi, minutes before midnight.
***Tryst*** enters English from Old French *triste* — **a hunting term: the appointed station
where you agreed to wait.** ⚠️ Verify against OED; this etymology is the point of the act.

**Gettysburg, 19 Nov 1863.** **268–272 words** (Bliss copy 272), about two minutes. Edward
Everett spoke ~2 hours before him, from memory, ~13,600 words, and was well received.
- **The envelope is a myth** — five copies survive in Lincoln's hand.
- **"Changed what the war was about"** is **Garry Wills's argument** (*Lincoln at Gettysburg*,
  Pulitzer 1993): the address reframed the war around the Declaration's promise of equality
  rather than around preserving the Union. ⚠️ A historian's reading, widely accepted, not a
  bare fact. State it as what the address *did*; never as Lincoln's stated intention.
- ⚠️ Lincoln did **not** originate "of the people, by the people, for the people" — similar
  constructions appear in Wycliffe and Theodore Parker.

## SCRIPT

**[0:00 — COLD OPEN]**

Somebody reaches for a word. And afterwards, the world is different.

It has happened over and over, in rooms nobody was watching.

*[beat]*

Sometimes it changes a thing. Sometimes a life. Sometimes a whole country.

Once, it changed what a war had been for.

---

**[0:16 — ONE · A WORD THAT CHANGED A THING]**

In 1920, a Czech playwright called Karel Čapek was writing a play about artificial workers
built to do the jobs nobody wanted.

He had a problem. There was no word for them, because there had never been any.

He tried *labori.* He hated it. So he went next door, where his brother Josef was painting,
and asked him what to call them.

Josef didn't look up from the canvas. He suggested the old Czech word for the grinding,
compulsory work a serf owed his lord.

***Robota.***

**Robot.**

*[beat]*

The play itself faded. It is hardly ever staged now, and hardly anyone reads it.

The word did not fade.

It is in Japanese, and Hindi, and Arabic, and Finnish. It is in the name of a factory
machine, a vacuum cleaner, a Mars rover and a hundred films that have never heard of Karel
Čapek. Every time anyone anywhere says it, they are using a word a painter offered his brother
over his shoulder, in one room, in one afternoon, in 1920.

One word. A hundred years. Every language on Earth.

---

**[1:19 — TWO · A WORD THAT CHANGED A LIFE]**

In the summer of 1772, a boy of about seventeen was working as a clerk on the island of St
Croix.

He had no money. He had almost no family left. And there was no reason on Earth to think he
would ever leave that island.

Then, on the thirty-first of August, a hurricane hit.

*[beat]*

He wrote a letter about it.

Not a plea. Not an appeal for help. Just an account of what the storm had done — the sound of
it, the ruin of it, the way it felt to be a small person underneath a very large sky.

Somebody showed the letter to the newspaper. The newspaper printed it.

And the people who read it were moved enough — by the writing, by a boy nobody had noticed
writing like that — to put money together and send him to America to be educated.

He described the weather, and strangers bought him an ocean.

*[beat]*

His name was Alexander Hamilton, and everything you have ever heard about him happens after
that letter. The revolution, the arguments, the bank, the duel. All of it downstream of a
seventeen-year-old on a ruined island, finding the words for a storm.

---

**[2:29 — THREE · A WORD THAT CHANGED A COUNTRY]**

Delhi, the night of the fourteenth of August, 1947.

It is minutes before midnight, and in minutes India will stop being ruled by somebody else for
the first time in almost two hundred years.

Jawaharlal Nehru stands up to speak.

**NEHRU:** At the stroke of the midnight hour, when the world sleeps, India will awake to life
and freedom.

*[beat]*

Now — the sentence before that one. He said that long years ago, India had made a ***tryst***
with destiny.

*Tryst.*

It's an odd word to reach for. It comes into English from Old French, and it started life as a
hunting term. It meant the appointed place. The spot where you agreed, in advance, to wait for
somebody.

He could have said *promise.* He could have said *appointment*, or *vow*, or *pledge*.

He picked the word that means **a place where you wait** — on a night his country had been
waiting for.

*[beat]*

That is what it looks like when somebody knows enough words to choose. Not a bigger word. The
*right* one — carrying a shade of meaning that no other word in the language was carrying.

Every schoolchild in India still learns that sentence.

---

**[3:37 — FOUR · A WORD THAT CHANGED WHAT A WAR MEANT]**

The nineteenth of November, 1863. A field in Pennsylvania where, four months earlier, around
fifty thousand men had been killed or wounded in three days.

They are dedicating a cemetery.

The main speaker is Edward Everett, the finest orator in America. He speaks for two hours,
from memory, thirteen thousand words. It is, by every account, very good.

Then Lincoln stands up. He has been invited to add — this is the actual wording — "a few
appropriate remarks."

He speaks for about two minutes. Two hundred and seventy-two words.

*[beat]*

And in those two minutes, he changes what the war is about.

Until Gettysburg, the war is to hold a country together. That is the official reason. That is
what those fifty thousand men were told.

Lincoln stands in the middle of their graves and says it is something else. That the country
was founded on a promise — that all men are created equal — and that it had never once kept
it. And that these men had died so that it could finally start.

A new birth of freedom.

*[beat]*

Nobody voted on that. There was no announcement. He simply said it, in two minutes, in a
field, and it became what that war had been for — and it has been ever since.

Two hundred and seventy-two words. Most of them one syllable long.

---

**[4:56 — CLOSE]**

A word from a painter. A letter from a boy on an island. One word chosen over another at
midnight. Two hundred and seventy-two words in a field.

A thing, a life, a country, and the meaning of a war.

*[beat]*

And every one of them already had the word when the moment came for it. Josef Čapek had
*robota.* Nehru had *tryst.* Lincoln had a whole language of short, old, plain words, and knew
exactly which ones would be believed.

You cannot reach for a word you do not have.

That is the entire reason any of this is worth learning. Not to sound clever — so that on the
day it matters, the right word is already there.

And you collect them one at a time.

Which is what a spelling bee is for.
