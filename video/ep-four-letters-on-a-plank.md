# Four Letters on a Plank

**Runtime:** ~9:14 — 1466 narration words at the 168.6 wpm episode two's voice track actually measured, plus ~24s between cues and a 9 s tail · **Voice:** the same narrator as episode one — warm, brisk, dry.
Two guest voices only.

**⚠️ WHY THIS IS A REWRITE.** The first draft was six acts, each teaching one "gear" of
English with a story attached. It was clever and it was cold. The diagnosis, in one line:
**the lessons were the spine and the people were the illustrations.** Frederick Douglass —
the most moving story available anywhere near this subject — was introduced with the words
"how he did it belongs in a spelling manual." That is a technique demo, not a film.

This version inverts it. **Douglass opens the film and closes it**, and the machinery of
English is taught in the middle, where it belongs — as the thing that was behind the door he
broke down. The gears are all still here. They are just no longer in charge.

**The controlling image:** a door. Some people are locked out of words. The key is always
smaller and more boring than anyone expects — four letters chalked on a plank, a word
shouted through a doorway by your brother, a page pulled back out of a wastebasket.

**The emotional rule for this script:** never tell the viewer what to feel, and never soften
a true thing to make it comfortable. The hardest beat in the film — that learning to read
made Douglass *miserable* before it made him free — is the one that earns everything else.
Cut it and this is a poster again.

---

## VOICES — two, not seven

The previous cut used seven guest voices and it was a costume party. Two survive, and only
because in each case a different voice *means* something rather than decorating something:

- **YOUNG DOUGLASS** — a child. The narrator cannot be a twelve-year-old boy daring another
  boy in the street, and that dare is the hinge of the whole film.
- **NEHRU** — an Indian voice for the moment India speaks. A British-inflected narrator
  reading that line is exactly the wrong sound for what is being described.

**Everything else is the narrator**, shifting register for the quote — which is what a
documentary narrator has always done. That includes Lincoln, Everett, Stephen King, Josef
Čapek and Mahalia Jackson. `prep2.py` reads speakers off the script, so moving a line
between narrator and guest is deleting or adding one `**SPEAKER:**` tag.

## THE FACTS (check every one before recording)

**Douglass in Baltimore.** Sent there about 1826, aged roughly eight. **Sophia Auld** began
teaching him the alphabet; her husband **Hugh Auld** stopped it, and said in the boy's
hearing that learning would make him unfit to be a slave and unhappy. Douglass calls this
moment a **"new and special revelation"** and says it showed him the white man's power to
enslave — ⚠️ he does *not*, in the Narrative, call it "the first anti-slavery speech I ever
heard"; that phrasing is later paraphrase. **The script paraphrases; do not put those words
in his mouth.**

**The shipyard.** Durgin and Bailey's yard, Fell's Point. Carpenters chalked **L** larboard,
**S** starboard, **F** forward, **A** aft onto timber. He learned those four, then challenged
boys who could write — *I bet you can't write better than that* — and copied what they
produced. He **traded bread** from the house to poor white children for lessons. He bought
**The Columbian Orator** for **fifty cents**.

**The misery.** This is the beat the film is built on and it is entirely his own account: he
writes that reading gave him "a view of my wretched condition, without the remedy," that it
tormented him, and that he sometimes envied his fellow slaves their ignorance and "often
wished myself a beast." ⚠️ Paraphrase closely; do not soften it into uplift.

**Teaching others.** He ran a **Sabbath school** for other enslaved people, at real risk to
himself. It was broken up by armed men.

**Later.** Douglass died **20 February 1895**, aged about 77. He published his own newspaper
(*The North Star*, from 1847), advised presidents, and was the **most photographed American
of the 19th century** — a deliberate choice, to control how a Black man was pictured.
⚠️ His exact birth year is uncertain (c. Feb 1818); say "about".

**Gettysburg, 19 Nov 1863.** Everett — the booked main speaker — ~2 hours from memory,
~13,600 words. Lincoln invited to add "a few appropriate remarks," spoke ~2 minutes,
**268–272 words** (Bliss copy 272). Everett's letter next day: "I should be glad if I could
flatter myself that I came as near to the central idea of the occasion, in two hours, as you
did in two minutes." **The envelope is a myth** — five copies survive in his hand.
⚠️ Lincoln did **not** originate "of the people, by the people, for the people."

**Hamilton.** Hurricane struck St Croix **31 Aug 1772**; his account published in the *Royal
Danish-American Gazette* **3 Oct 1772**; subscribers raised funds to send him to North
America. ⚠️ Scholarship differs on the mechanics — say the letter was **part of** what got
him off the island, not the sole cause.

**Norman doublets.** cow/**beef**, pig/**pork**, sheep/**mutton**, calf/**veal**. ⚠️
Popularised by **Walter Scott, *Ivanhoe* (1819)** — on-screen card, not a narrated line. The
split hardened over centuries; not a clean 1066 switch.

**Silent K.** *knight, knee, know* — pronounced in Old and Middle English, silent by the
17th century. ⚠️ *knight*'s *gh* was also a real sound.

**Greek *ph*.** photograph = *phos/photo* (light) + *graphein* (to write).

**Robot.** Čapek's *R.U.R.*, published 1920. He credited his brother **Josef** with the word,
from Czech ***robota*** — the forced labour a serf owed. He first tried *labori* and hated it.

**Shakespeare.** OED credits ~1,700 earliest *recorded* uses. ⚠️ **Recorded ≠ invented**, and
the script says so.

**Stan Lee.** Peter Parker, Bruce Banner, Reed Richards, Sue Storm, Stephen Strange, Matt
Murdock. By his own account his memory was poor and the repeated initial helped.

**Stephen King.** "The road to hell is paved with adverbs" — ⚠️ verify wording against the
book. He threw the opening pages of *Carrie* in the wastebasket; **Tabitha** retrieved them.

**Nehru, 14 Aug 1947**, Constituent Assembly, minutes before midnight. ***Tryst*** enters
English from Old French *triste* — **a hunting term: the appointed station where you agreed
to wait.** ⚠️ Verify against OED; this etymology is why the beat exists.

**King, 28 Aug 1963.** ~250,000 present, carried live by all three networks. The famous
passage **was not in the prepared text**; **Mahalia Jackson** called out to him about the
dream. ⚠️ But he had used versions of it before — Detroit June 1963, Rocky Mount Nov 1962.
**Do not flatten this into "he improvised it."**

## SCRIPT

---

**[0:00 — COLD OPEN · THE ROOM IN BALTIMORE]**

In Baltimore, in 1826, a woman began teaching a boy of about eight his letters.

She was the wife of the man who owned him.

When her husband found out he stopped it — and explained why out loud, in the room, with the
child standing right there. Reading would ruin the boy, he said. It would make him unhappy
for the rest of his life. It would make him unfit to be a slave.

*[beat]*

The boy was listening.

And he wrote, years later, that it came to him like a revelation — because the man had just
explained, without meaning to, exactly where the door was.

The boy's name was Frederick Douglass.

*[beat]*

This is a film about words. What they open, and what they cost.

---

**[0:48 — ACT ONE · FOUR LETTERS]**

He wasn't allowed to be taught. So he stole it.

He was sent to work at a shipyard on the Baltimore waterfront, where the carpenters chalked
letters onto the timbers to mark where each piece belonged on the ship. **L** for larboard. **S** for starboard. **F** for forward. **A** for aft.

Four letters. He learned those four.

Then he'd find a boy in the street who could write, and say —

**YOUNG DOUGLASS:** I bet you can't write better than that.

And the boy, being a boy, would prove that he absolutely could. And Douglass would copy down
the proof, and keep it.

He traded bread out of the kitchen for lessons, because he had food and the children in the
street did not. And when he had saved fifty cents he bought a second-hand book of famous
speeches, and took it apart line by line to find out how the sentences were doing it.

*[beat]*

And here is the part that gets left out.

It made him miserable.

Because now he could read what was written about people like him. Now he could name, exactly,
what was being done to him and to his mother and to everyone he had ever known. He wrote that
it had shown him his condition without showing him any way out of it — that there were days
he envied the other men their ignorance, and wished himself an animal.

Learning to read did not set him free. First, it told him precisely how unfree he was.

He kept going anyway. And then he began teaching others — in secret, on Sundays, knowing what
would happen if they were found.

Because a door is not much use if only one person walks through it.

---

**[2:34 — ACT TWO · WHAT WAS BEHIND IT]**

So what was on the other side of that door, that was worth all of it?

English. Which is, frankly, a mess.

It isn't one language. It's three, standing on each other's shoulders in a long coat. Old
English at the bottom — blunt, short, and it owns your body and your family. French upstairs
after 1066, when the Normans arrive and the people running the country stop speaking it. Then
Latin and Greek over both.

You can still see the seam — on the menu.

In the field it's a **cow**. On the plate, **beef**. **Pig**, **pork**. **Sheep**, **mutton**.

The same animal twice, in two languages, because for three hundred years the people who
looked after it spoke English and the people who ate it spoke French.

*[beat]*

Why is there a K in **knight**? Because we used to say it. *K-nicht.* That K isn't silent —
it's *extinct.* A sound people stopped making four hundred years ago, still standing in the
spelling like a footprint set in concrete.

Why does **photograph** have a *ph* and not an honest F? Because it's Greek, and it's telling
you so. *Photo* — light. *Graph* — writing.

Every awkward spelling in English is somebody's footprint. The beef on the menu is an army
that never went home. English is spelled the way it is because English is a record of
everyone who ever arrived.

That's what makes it hard. It's also what makes it worth the trouble. Nothing else on your
desk is a museum.

---

**[4:07 — ACT THREE · PEOPLE WHO NEEDED A WORD]**

In 1772 a seventeen-year-old clerk on the island of St Croix had no money, no family to speak
of, and no way off.

Then a hurricane hit.

He wrote a letter about it — just a description of what the storm had done. Someone showed it
to the newspaper. The newspaper printed it. And the people who read it put money together to
send the boy who wrote it to school in America.

He described the weather, and it carried him across an ocean. His name was Alexander
Hamilton.

*[beat]*

Sometimes the word you need doesn't exist yet.

In 1920 a Czech playwright, Karel Čapek, was writing a play about artificial workers and was
stuck for what to call them. He tried *labori*. Awful. So he went next door, where his
brother Josef was painting, and asked. Josef didn't look up. He suggested the old Czech word
for the grinding work a serf owed his lord — *robota.*

**Robot.**

One playwright, one play, one word — borrowed from a man who didn't stop painting to say it,
and a century later in nearly every language on Earth.

That's not genius arriving like weather. It's a man asking his brother a question.

---

**[5:22 — ACT FOUR · WHEN IT LANDS]**

On the nineteenth of November, 1863, two men spoke in the same field on the same afternoon.

The first was Edward Everett, and he was *good* — the most celebrated speaker in America,
booked as the main event. Two hours, from memory, thirteen thousand words. Lincoln had been
invited to add, and this is the real wording, "a few appropriate remarks."

Lincoln's few remarks came to two hundred and seventy-two words. *Of the people, by the
people, for the people.* Every word in that line is Old English. All one syllable.

That isn't Lincoln running out of vocabulary. It's Lincoln knowing that when you want to be
believed you reach for the short old words. The long ones are for explaining.

The next morning Everett sat down and wrote to Lincoln. *I should be glad if I could flatter
myself that I came as near to the central idea of the occasion, in two hours, as you did in
two minutes.*

Two hours. Two minutes. Everett knew exactly what had happened to him.

---

**[6:27 — ACT FIVE · MIDNIGHT, AND THE STEPS]**

Delhi, the night of the fourteenth of August, 1947. Minutes to midnight, and India is minutes
from independence. Jawaharlal Nehru stands up.

**NEHRU:** At the stroke of the midnight hour, when the world sleeps, India will awake to
life and freedom.

Look at the word he'd chosen a sentence earlier: India had made a ***tryst*** with destiny.
*Tryst* comes into English from Old French, where it began as a hunting word — the appointed
place where you agreed to wait.

A waiting place, at midnight, at the birth of a nation. He could have said *promise.*

*[beat]*

Sixteen years later, Washington. A quarter of a million people on the Mall, every television
network carrying it live, and Martin Luther King Jr on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial —
measuring out a hundred years in Lincoln's own phrasing, with Lincoln's statue behind him.

And the most famous passage in that speech was not in the script.

He was working through a prepared text. Behind him, the gospel singer Mahalia Jackson called
out to him — *tell them about the dream.* He put the pages aside.

But he wasn't making it up. He had been building that passage for a year, in halls of a few
hundred people, where nobody was watching.

So when a quarter of a million people went quiet, it was ready.

That's what improvisation actually is. Preparation you can reach without looking.

---

**[7:53 — ACT SIX · THE MAN ON THE PLATFORM]**

Frederick Douglass lived to be about seventy-seven. He published his own newspaper. He
advised presidents. He sat for more photographs than any other American of the nineteenth
century, deliberately, because he understood exactly what a picture of him did.

All of it started with four letters chalked onto a plank by a carpenter who had no idea he
was teaching anybody anything.

*[beat]*

So — why learn to spell, in a world with spell-check?

Not one person in this film was handed it. Lincoln rewrote his two minutes five times. Stan
Lee gave half of Marvel the same initial twice because he kept forgetting his own characters'
names. Stephen King threw the first pages of *Carrie* in the bin; his wife took them back out.

They all went and looked inside the machine. Spelling is the door in.

Learn that **photograph** has a *ph* because it's Greek for light, and you haven't memorised a
spelling — you've been handed *photon*, *photosynthesis*, *telephoto* and a hundred more,
free, for life.

That's the trade. Every hard word you take apart hands you the next twenty.

The rest is practice.

Which is what a spelling bee is for.

## WHAT CHANGED FROM THE FIRST DRAFT, AND WHY

| | First draft | This draft |
|---|---|---|
| Spine | six lessons, with people as examples | one person, with lessons inside his story |
| Douglass | act five of six, framed as "belongs in a spelling manual" | opens the film and closes it |
| Opening | a rhetorical puzzle (two speakers, two hours, two minutes) | a room in Baltimore, a child, and a man explaining the way out by accident |
| Hardest beat | none — nothing cost anyone anything | reading made Douglass miserable before it freed him |
| Guest voices | seven | two |
| Stan Lee | his own act | one clause in the close, where it does more work |

**Restored from the cuts:** Hamilton's hurricane letter. It is the purest "words got me out"
story available and it belongs beside Douglass, not on a spare list.

**Still cut:** Raphael Lemkin and *genocide*. The film now carries its weight in Act One
instead, and it does not need a second grave beat.

**The line that must not be cut:** *"Learning to read did not set him free. First, it told him
precisely how unfree he was."* Everything else in the film is available in a hundred other
places. That is the sentence that makes this worth making, and it is Douglass's own account,
not an invention.

## OPEN QUESTIONS

1. **Title.** *Four Letters on a Plank* — concrete, and it pays off twice. The old title,
   *Nobody Is Born Good With Words*, is still available and is more obviously about spelling.
2. **Two guest voices, or three?** Mahalia Jackson calling across the crowd is the one other
   place a second voice would earn itself. Say the word and it goes back in.
3. **The Baltimore opening** names slavery in the first thirty seconds, plainly, for an
   audience of eight-to-fifteens. I think that is right — it is the truth and children can
   hold it — but it is a bigger call than anything in episode one.
