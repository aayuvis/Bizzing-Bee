# Four Letters on a Plank

**Runtime:** ~9:31 — 1512 narration words at 168.6 wpm (the rate episode two's voice track actually measured) plus ~24s between cues and a 9 s tail · **Voice:** the narrator from episode one — warm, brisk, dry.
**Two guest voices only.**

---

## THE MESSAGE

> **Words are a leader's tool. They move hearts, and they turn good into great — and nobody
> is handed them. You study them.**

Four claims. Every beat serves at least one, and no beat exists that serves none.

**1 · It's critical to study words.** The spine, never stated as advice. It works because
*everyone in this film studied*, and that is the part their legends leave out. Douglass bought
a book of speeches for fifty cents and took it apart line by line. Lincoln rewrote his two
minutes five times. King had been building "the dream" for a year in rooms of three hundred
people. Stephen King threw the first pages of *Carrie* in the bin. The film never tells anyone
to study — it makes the studying visible underneath what looks like genius.

**2 · Words are the tools of leaders.** Shown as tools: chosen, weighed, swapped. Nehru could
have said *promise*. He said *tryst*.

**3 · Words move hearts.** Proven by effects on real people, never by adjectives. Everett
writes a letter of surrender the next morning. Strangers on St Croix put money together for a
boy who described a storm. A quarter of a million people go quiet.

**4 · Words turn good into great.** Everett was not a windbag — he was the finest speaker in
America and he was *good* that day. Lincoln was *great*. Same field, same afternoon, same
crowd; the only variable was the words. Act Four exists to dramatise this and nothing else.

**THE TEST FOR EVERY BEAT:** *what changed because of these words, and what did this person do
to be ready?* A beat that only demonstrates "wasn't that well put" is cut. That test is what
removed the original opening, which was a craft lesson about brevity.

**THE CLOSING OFFER**, a consequence rather than an instruction: *you cannot reach for a word
you don't have.*

## VOICES — two

Seven guest voices was a costume party. Two survive, each because a different voice *means*
something rather than decorating something:

- **YOUNG DOUGLASS** — a child. The narrator cannot be a twelve-year-old daring another boy in
  the street, and that dare is the hinge of the film.
- **NEHRU** — an Indian voice for the moment India speaks.

Everything else is the narrator shifting register for the quote, which is what documentary
narrators have always done: Lincoln, Everett, Josef Čapek, Mahalia Jackson. `prep2.py` reads
speakers off this script, so moving a line between narrator and guest is adding or deleting one
`**SPEAKER:**` tag.

## THE FACTS (check every one before recording)

**Douglass in Baltimore.** Sent there about 1826, aged roughly eight. **Sophia Auld** began
teaching him his letters; her husband **Hugh Auld** stopped it, saying in the boy's hearing that
learning would make him unfit to be a slave and unhappy. Douglass calls it a **"new and special
revelation."** ⚠️ He does *not* call it "the first anti-slavery speech I ever heard" — that is
later paraphrase. The script paraphrases; do not put those words in his mouth.

**The shipyard.** Durgin and Bailey's, Fell's Point. Carpenters chalked **L** larboard, **S**
starboard, **F** forward, **A** aft. He learned those four, challenged boys who could write — *I
bet you can't write better than that* — and copied the proof. He **traded bread** for lessons
and bought **The Columbian Orator** for **fifty cents**.

**The misery.** Entirely his own account: reading gave him "a view of my wretched condition,
without the remedy," it tormented him, and he sometimes envied other slaves their ignorance and
"often wished myself a beast." ⚠️ Paraphrase closely. **Do not soften it into uplift.**

**Teaching others.** A **Sabbath school** for other enslaved people, at real risk. Broken up by
armed men.

**Later.** Died **20 Feb 1895**, about 77. Published *The North Star* from 1847, advised
presidents, **most photographed American of the 19th century** — deliberately, to control how a
Black man was pictured. ⚠️ Birth year uncertain (c. Feb 1818): say "about."

**Gettysburg, 19 Nov 1863.** Everett — booked main speaker, ~2 hours from memory, ~13,600 words,
and **genuinely well received**. The film's claim that he was *good* is not a concession, it is
the record. Lincoln invited to add "a few appropriate remarks," spoke ~2 minutes, **268–272
words** (Bliss copy 272).
- Everett's letter, 20 Nov: "I should be glad if I could flatter myself that I came as near to
  the central idea of the occasion, in two hours, as you did in two minutes."
- **The envelope is a myth** — five copies survive in his hand.
- **"Changed what the war was about."** This is Garry Wills's argument in *Lincoln at
  Gettysburg* (Pulitzer, 1993) — that Lincoln reframed the war around the Declaration's promise
  of equality rather than around preserving the Union. ⚠️ A **historian's reading**, widely
  accepted but not a bare fact. The script states it as what the address *did*, which is fair;
  do not attribute it to Lincoln as a stated intention.

**Hamilton.** Hurricane struck St Croix **31 Aug 1772**; account published in the *Royal
Danish-American Gazette* **3 Oct 1772**; subscribers raised funds to send him to North America.
⚠️ Scholarship differs — the letter was **part of** what got him off the island, not provably
the sole cause.

**Norman doublets.** cow/**beef**, pig/**pork**, sheep/**mutton**. ⚠️ Popularised by **Walter
Scott, *Ivanhoe* (1819)** — on-screen card, not a narrated line. The split hardened over
centuries; not a clean 1066 switch.

**Silent K.** *knight, knee, know* — pronounced in Old and Middle English, silent by the 17th
century. ⚠️ *knight*'s *gh* was also a real sound.

**Greek *ph*.** photograph = *phos/photo* (light) + *graphein* (to write).

**Robot.** Čapek's *R.U.R.*, published 1920. He credited his brother **Josef** with the word,
from Czech ***robota*** — the forced labour a serf owed. He first tried *labori* and hated it.
⚠️ In the TTS text spell it **Robota** and put **no phonetic hint in the direction** — giving
the model two spellings of one word produced neither.

**Stephen King.** Threw the opening pages of *Carrie* in the wastebasket; **Tabitha** retrieved
them and told him to go on.

**Nehru, 14 Aug 1947**, Constituent Assembly, minutes before midnight. ***Tryst*** enters
English from Old French *triste* — **a hunting term: the appointed station where you agreed to
wait.** ⚠️ Verify against OED; this etymology is why the beat exists.

**King, 28 Aug 1963.** ~250,000 present, all three networks live. The famous passage **was not
in the prepared text**; **Mahalia Jackson** called out to him. ⚠️ But he had used versions
before — Detroit June 1963, Rocky Mount Nov 1962. **Do not flatten this into "he improvised
it."** The whole point is that he had studied it.

## SCRIPT

---

**[0:00 — COLD OPEN · THE ROOM IN BALTIMORE]**

In Baltimore, in 1826, a woman began teaching a boy of about eight his letters.

She was the wife of the man who owned him.

When her husband found out he stopped it — and he explained why out loud, in the room, with the
child standing right there. Reading would ruin the boy, he said. It would make him unfit to be a slave, and unhappy for
the rest of his life.

*[beat]*

The boy was listening.

And he wrote, years later, that it came to him like a revelation — because a man had just
explained to him, without meaning to, exactly where the door was.

The boy's name was Frederick Douglass.

*[beat]*

This is a film about words — what they open, what they cost, and the unglamorous thing
everyone who ever changed anything with them did first.

---

**[0:52 — ACT ONE · FOUR LETTERS]**

He wasn't allowed to be taught. So he stole it.

He was sent to work at a Baltimore shipyard, where the carpenters chalked letters onto the
timbers to mark where each piece belonged on the ship. **L** for larboard.
**S** for starboard. **F** for forward. **A** for aft.

Four letters. He learned those four.

Then he'd find a boy in the street who could write, and say —

**YOUNG DOUGLASS:** I bet you can't write better than that.

And the boy, being a boy, would prove that he absolutely could — and Douglass copied down the
proof and kept it.

He traded bread out of the kitchen for lessons. And when he had saved fifty cents he bought a
second-hand book of famous speeches, and took it apart line by line to work out how the
sentences were doing it.

*[beat]*

And here is the part that gets left out.

It made him miserable.

Because now he could read what was written about people like him. Now he could name, exactly,
what was being done to him and to his mother and to everyone he had ever known. He wrote that
it showed him his condition without showing him any way out of it — that there were days he
envied the other men their ignorance, and wished himself an animal.

Learning to read did not set him free. First it told him precisely how unfree he was.

He kept going anyway. And then he began teaching others — in secret, on Sundays, knowing
exactly what would happen if they were found. Men with clubs came and broke it up.

Because a door is not much use if only one person walks through it.

---

**[2:36 — ACT TWO · WHAT WAS BEHIND IT]**

So what was on the other side of that door, that was worth all of that?

English. Which is, frankly, a mess. It isn't one language — it's three, standing on each
other's shoulders in a long coat. Old English at the bottom. French upstairs after 1066, when
the Normans arrive and the people running the country stop speaking it. Latin and Greek over
both.

You can still see the seam. It's on the menu.

In the field it's a **cow**. On the plate, **beef**. **Pig**, **pork**. **Sheep**, **mutton**.

The same animal twice, in two languages, because for three hundred years the people who looked
after it spoke English and the people who ate it spoke French.

*[beat]*

Why is there a K in **knight**? Because we used to say it. *K-nicht.* That K isn't silent —
it's *extinct*, still standing in the spelling four hundred years after the last person said
it.

Every awkward spelling in English is somebody's footprint. The beef on the menu is an army
that never went home. English is spelled the way it is because English is a record of everyone
who ever arrived.

That's what makes it hard. It's also what makes it learnable, because none of it is random.

---

**[3:53 — ACT THREE · WHAT A WORD CAN DO]**

In 1772 a seventeen-year-old clerk on the island of St Croix had no money, no family to speak
of, and no way off.

Then a hurricane hit.

He wrote a letter about it — just a description of what the storm had done. Someone showed it
to the newspaper. The newspaper printed it. And the people who read it were moved enough to put
money together and send the boy who wrote it to school in America.

He described the weather, and strangers bought him an ocean crossing. His name was Alexander
Hamilton.

*[beat]*

And sometimes the word a leader needs doesn't exist yet.

In 1920 a Czech playwright, Karel Čapek, was writing a play about artificial workers and was
stuck for what to call them. He tried *labori.* Awful. So he went next door, where his brother
Josef was painting, and asked.

Josef didn't look up. He offered the old Czech word for the grinding work a serf owed his lord
— *robota.*

**Robot.**

One word, handed over by a man who didn't stop painting to say it — and a century later it's in
nearly every language on Earth.

---

**[5:04 — ACT FOUR · GOOD, AND GREAT]**

On the nineteenth of November, 1863, two men spoke in the same field, on the same afternoon.

The first was Edward Everett, and here is what everyone gets wrong about him. He was not a
windbag. He was the finest speaker in America, the booked main event, and by every account he
was **good** that day — two hours, from memory, thirteen thousand words, and people listened to
all of it.

Then Lincoln stood up, invited to add — this is the real wording — "a few appropriate
remarks."

He spoke for about two minutes. Two hundred and seventy-two words.

And in those two minutes he changed what the war was about.

Before Gettysburg, the war was to hold a country together. Lincoln stood in a field of fresh
graves and said it was something else — that the country had been built on a promise it never
kept, and these men had died so it could start keeping it. A new birth of freedom.

Nobody voted on that. He said it, and it became what the war had been for, and it has been
ever since.

The next morning Everett sat down and wrote to him. *I should be glad if I could flatter myself
that I came as near to the central idea of the occasion, in two hours, as you did in two
minutes.*

Good, and great. Same field, same afternoon, same crowd.

The difference was the words. And Lincoln had written his five times.

---

**[6:35 — ACT FIVE · MIDNIGHT, AND THE STEPS]**

Delhi, the night of the fourteenth of August, 1947. Minutes to midnight, and India is minutes
from independence. Jawaharlal Nehru stands up.

**NEHRU:** At the stroke of the midnight hour, when the world sleeps, India will awake to life
and freedom.

Now look at the word he had chosen a sentence earlier: India had made a ***tryst*** with
destiny. *Tryst* comes into English from Old French, where it began as a hunting word — the
appointed place where you agreed to wait.

A waiting place, at midnight, at the birth of a nation. He could have said *promise.* He had
the better word to reach for.

*[beat]*

Sixteen years later, Washington. A quarter of a million people on the Mall, every network
carrying it live, and Martin Luther King Jr on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial, measuring out
a hundred years in Lincoln's own phrasing.

And the most famous passage in that speech was not in the script.

He was working through a prepared text. Behind him the gospel singer Mahalia Jackson called out
— *tell them about the dream.* He put the pages aside.

But he wasn't making it up. He had been building that passage for a year, in halls of three
hundred people, where nobody was watching. So when a quarter of a million went quiet, it was
ready.

That is what improvisation actually is. Preparation you can reach without looking.

---

**[8:02 — ACT SIX · THE MAN ON THE PLATFORM]**

Frederick Douglass lived to be about seventy-seven. He published his own newspaper, advised
presidents, and sat for more photographs than any other American of the nineteenth century —
deliberately, because he understood exactly what a picture of him did.

All of it started with four letters chalked onto a plank by a carpenter who had no idea he was
teaching anybody anything.

*[beat]*

Not one person in this film was handed it. Lincoln rewrote his two minutes five times. Douglass
took a fifty-cent book apart line by line. King spent a year in small rooms building six
minutes.

Every one of them studied. That is the boring secret underneath all of it.

And it starts with spelling, because a word you can spell is a word you have looked at long
enough to understand. Learn that **photograph** has a *ph* because it's Greek for light, and
you haven't memorised a spelling — you've been handed *photon*, *photosynthesis*, *telephoto*
and a hundred more, free, for life.

Every hard word you take apart hands you the next twenty.

And one day you will want to say something that matters, to someone who matters, and you will
need the exact right word. You cannot reach for a word you don't have.

Which is what a spelling bee is for.

## HOW THIS DRAFT DIFFERS

| | Previous | This |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Two speakers, two hours, two minutes — a **craft lesson about brevity** | A room in Baltimore, a child, and a man explaining the way out by accident |
| Gettysburg | "Reach for the short old words" — a writing tip | **Good and great.** Everett was good; Lincoln changed what the war was about |
| Everett | a man who ran long | the finest speaker in America, genuinely good that day — which is what makes the comparison mean anything |
| Spine | six lessons with people attached | **study**, made visible under every story |
| Close | "nobody was born with it" | "every one of them studied", then *you cannot reach for a word you don't have* |
| Guest voices | seven | two |

**Cut and staying cut:** Shakespeare's seventeen hundred words (Čapek already carries "a
playwright makes a word", and the beat was a caveat about a myth, not a story). Stephen King's
adverbs aphorism (he is stronger in the close, with the wastebasket). Stan Lee's alliteration
(charming, but it demonstrates a trick rather than a change). Raphael Lemkin and *genocide*
(Act One now carries the film's weight).

**The two lines that must survive any edit:**
- *"Learning to read did not set him free. First it told him precisely how unfree he was."*
- *"Good, and great. Same field, same afternoon, same crowd. The difference was the words."*
