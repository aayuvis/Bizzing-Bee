# GET BROMIC
### Brand campaign concept — evaluation and development

---

## 0. Verdict up front

**Green light, with four conditions.**

The concept is strategically sound for a reason that has nothing to do with whether the
jokes land: Bromic competes in a category where **no brand is distinctive**, and where the
buyer enters with **no brand in mind**. Outdoor heating is an infrequent, considered,
largely unbranded purchase — most homeowners arrive at it via a builder, a designer, or a
showroom counter, and take whatever is recommended. In that situation the brand that is
*remembered at the moment the drawing is being marked up* wins, and every competitor is
currently spending their money to be forgotten in exactly the same way.

Humour is the only unclaimed territory in this category. "Get Bromic" is a better idea
than it looks on paper because it isn't a joke — it's a **format**, and formats are what
build fame cheaply over years.

The four conditions are in §6. The most important one, and the one that will decide
whether this works: **every execution must be a joke about the cost of *not* having it,
not a joke about being cold.** More on that in §4.

## 1. Where Bromic actually sits today

### The brand

| | |
|---|---|
| Origin | Australian; design-led, positions as "built by designers, for designers" |
| Core tech | Smart-Heat™ infrared; the wind-resistance claim is their genuine technical differentiator |
| Range | Gas, electric and portable — **the only one of the big three that does gas** |
| Collections | Platinum (sleek, ceramic glass + stainless), Tungsten (industrial), Cobalt, Eclipse |
| Price ladder | roughly **$674 → $4,000+** at retail, before install |
| Current line | *"Extending unforgettable outdoor moments with refined heating solutions"* |
| Campaign language | "Extend Your Outdoor Season", "Extend the Moment", "Bring Warmth With Bromic" |
| Trade posture | Dedicated architects & designers hub; specification, mounting and CEU-style content |

### The creative convention they're trapped in

Their own marketing and everybody else's runs the identical film: golden hour, slow dolly,
infinity pool, cashmere throw, two wine glasses, laughter with no dialogue, product beauty
shot, "extend your season." Swap the logo at the end and the ad works for Infratech,
Heatscope, or a teak furniture brand. That is the definition of a category with **zero
distinctive assets** — and it means every dollar of media Bromic spends is partly building
the category for whoever the customer happens to meet at the showroom.

Your read — *"very posh, high end and same-same, just like any other luxury brand"* — is
correct, and it is the strategic opening.

### The competitive set

- **Infratech** — US-manufactured, electric only, flush-mount and control options, cheaper.
  The default spec on a lot of covered patios. Will counter a fame play on price and
  "Made in USA."
- **Heatscope** — German, Red Dot-winning (Pure, Bjorn Blisse), design-purist. Small but
  credible with architects.
- **Bromic's real edge** — gas (which Infratech simply cannot do), wind resistance, and the
  breadth to cover residential *and* hospitality on one spec sheet.

## 2. The strategic idea

**"Extend the Moment" is the right promise and it stays.** It is the brand's equity, it is
what the product genuinely delivers, and it is where every one of these films resolves to.
Nothing here replaces it.

What a promise cannot do on its own is create urgency. It asks the buyer to want *more* of
something already pleasant — a soft ask against a $2,000–$6,000 installed line item, and the
reason this category gets deferred to next year, every year.

So Get Bromic supplies the other half. Not a new promise — an **argument** underneath the one
that already works: the cost of *not*.

> **You didn't spend eighty thousand dollars on a backyard to look at it through glass.**

That argument does the commercial work. It moves the purchase out of the
"heater" category — where it is a commodity, price-shopped against a $300 mushroom heater
from a big-box store — and into the **"protecting the investment you already made"**
category, where $4,000 is a rounding error on an $80k outdoor build and the comparison set
is the pergola, the outdoor kitchen, and the pool.

That is also precisely the argument that works on the trade. An architect doesn't want to
hear that heat is nice. They want to hear that an unheated outdoor room is **unused square
footage they designed and will get blamed for.**

**Endline:** `Get Bromic.`

For the first flight, lock it up with a descriptor — `Get Bromic. Outdoor heating.` —
because a chunk of the target does not know the brand *or* that this product category
exists in built-in form. Drop the descriptor once aided awareness clears ~35%.

---

## 3. Why "Get Bromic" is the right vehicle

1. **It's a verb-brand.** Imperative + brand name is the most durable endline structure
   there is, because it works as a punchline, a sign-off, a CTA, a hashtag, and a thing a
   salesperson can say out loud in a showroom.
2. **It's a renewable format, not a campaign.** `[absurd consequence of no heat] → Get
   Bromic.` You can write that forever. The closest working analogue is Specsavers'
   *"Should've gone to Specsavers"* — same structure, ran two decades, built enormous fame
   in an equally low-interest category. Also in the family: *Got Milk?*, *Dumb Ways to
   Die*.
3. **The insight is genuinely funny, and genuinely theirs.** Sunk-cost misery — the
   beautiful thing you paid for and cannot use — is universal, specific to this product,
   and completely absent from the category's advertising.
4. **It's cheap to produce at volume.** Every execution is one structure: cold misery →
   warm turn → endline. One house, one director, one shoot, six films, twenty statics. See
   §8.

## 4. The discipline filter (the single most important page here)

Your two seed ideas are not equally strong, and the difference between them is the rule
the whole platform depends on.

| Seed | Joke is about | Ownable by Bromic? |
|---|---|---|
| **$80k backyard sitting like a wasteland** | wasted money / unused asset | ✅ Yes — only a premium built-in brand can tell this joke |
| **Don't sleep inside a horse** | being cold | ❌ No — any heater brand, at any price, could run it |

**The filter: if the joke would still work for a $300 big-box patio heater, kill it.**

Cold jokes are free for everyone and they drag Bromic down-market — they position the
product as a remedy for discomfort, which is a commodity job at a commodity price. Wasted-
asset jokes are only available to the brand that lives on architects' drawings, and they
justify the premium in the same breath as they get the laugh.

The horse idea isn't dead — it's the funniest line in your brief and it should survive. It
just needs to be rebuilt so the victim is the *lifestyle*, not the temperature. Execution 2
below does that.

## 5. Executions

### The format (30s)

| Time | Beat |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:03 | **Hook + attribution.** A stunning architectural exterior, held still. The Bromic unit is visible in frame, unlit. |
| 0:03–0:20 | **The bit.** Deadpan escalation. One location. Dry narrator or minimal dialogue. |
| 0:20–0:26 | **The turn.** The heater clicks on — *audio mnemonic* — amber bloom fills the frame, people flood back outside. |
| 0:26–0:30 | **Endline card.** `Get Bromic.` + logo + URL. |

Cutdowns: 30s / 15s / 6s, in 16:9, 1:1 and 9:16. The 6s is the turn only, with the endline.

A **single deadpan voice artist across every film** is the cheapest distinctive asset you
will ever buy. Cast it once, use it for five years.

---

### 1. "Wasteland" — the hero *(your idea; make this the launch film)*

Late autumn. An immaculate $80k outdoor kitchen and lounge. Leaves collecting on the
Corian. A pool float slowly deflating. Inside, at the kitchen window, the owner stands with
a mug, watching it the way a widow watches the sea.

> **VO:** "The Hendersons' outdoor kitchen. Forty-one thousand dollars. Eleven months of
> the year, it's a shrine."

**Turn:** heater on, the doors open, twelve people spill outside, the space is *full*.

> **Get Bromic.**

---

### 2. "Don't Do It, Gary" — the horse, rebuilt

A man alone in a $12,000 outdoor lounge chair at dusk, wrapped in three blankets, teeth
audibly chattering. Slowly, his eyes drift to the neighbour's paddock. A pony. He looks at
the pony. The pony looks at him. A terrible calm settles over his face. He begins,
fractionally, to stand.

Cut to black.

> **VO:** "Don't do it, Gary."
>
> **Get Bromic.**

Restraint is what makes this funnier than the literal version, and it solves three problems
at once: no gore in a luxury brand's feed, no recreation of protectable footage from *The
Revenant*, no animal-welfare read. **Never name the film, never recreate a specific shot,
never touch the score.** Parody is defensible; imitation of protectable elements is not.

*Flag: this is the highest-variance execution in the set. Test it — some of the 40–70
female segment, who very often controls this decision, may find it merely strange. Run it
as film #3 or #4, not the launch.*

---

### 3. "Migration" — the travel-anywhere one

Nature-documentary treatment. Slow motion. A dinner party, as the sun drops, migrating
indoors — glasses in hand, shoulders rising, a slow herd movement toward the kitchen
island.

> **VO (hushed, Attenborough register):** "As the temperature falls, the herd abandons the
> terrace and returns to the island. Here they will remain, pressed together, for the next
> five hours. The terrace will not be seen again until May."

**Turn:** heat on, the herd flows back out.

> **Get Bromic.**

This is the most exportable film in the set — it works in the US, Australia, and the UK
without a rewrite, and the "everyone crammed in the kitchen" truth is devastatingly
recognisable to the target.

---

### 4. "The Pergola" — for the remodel audience

A couple gives four friends the house tour. Beautiful. Everything lands.

They arrive at the glass doors. Beyond them, in the freezing dark, a magnificent cedar
pergola.

> **Host:** "And this is the pergola."

Six people stand at the glass looking at it. Nobody opens the door. Nobody moves. A very
long beat.

> **Host:** "...We're going to do something with it."
>
> **Get Bromic.**

The sharpest film for your remodel/add-on segment, and the one custom builders will
forward to each other.

---

### 5. "Layers" — for the design-conscious decision maker

A woman dressing for her own dinner party. Silk dress. Then thermals under it. Then a
puffer. Then a scarf. Then a blanket. Then a beanie. Then a fleece from the mudroom that is
visibly not hers and not anyone's. She sits at an exquisitely styled table looking like a
bin bag. Her guests, identically upholstered, stare back.

> **Turn:** the same table, the same night — the dress alone.
>
> **VO:** "Dress for your table. Not the weather."
>
> **Get Bromic.**

---

### 6. "You'll Hear About It" — the trade cut

A custom builder walks the client through the finished build. Golden hour, everything
perfect.

> **Client:** "It's flawless."

**SUPER: SIX MONTHS LATER.** The same terrace, empty, dark, rain on the stone. Cut to the
builder's phone, ringing, at 9:40pm. The builder looks at the screen. The builder does not
pick up.

> **VO:** "Specify heat. Or you'll hear about it."
>
> **Get Bromic.**

This is how the platform stretches to B2B without diluting: same endline, same format, but
the victim shifts from the homeowner to the **builder's reputation** — which is the only
currency that moves that audience.

---

### Statics

The art direction stays exactly as luxurious as Bromic's current work. **The tension
between the exquisite photograph and the blunt caption is the joke.** Set the line in their
existing typography, large, with nothing else on the frame but the logo and `Get Bromic.`

- Immaculate empty patio in the rain — **"Your second living room. Closed for the season."**
- Outdoor kitchen, unused, autumn light — **"$80,000 backyard. $4 hot chocolate. Kitchen."**
- Wide, beautiful, empty terrace — **"Six months a year, this is a very expensive painting."**
- Cedar pergola at dusk — **"Pergola *(n.)*: a structure for looking at."**
- Twelve coats on a bannister — **"The outdoor dining set is beautiful. Here's where everyone ate."**
- Two chairs, one throw, both empty — **"Seats twelve. Seats zero in October."**
- Pool at night, steam, no people — **"Heated pool. Unheated everything else."**

Ship ~20 of these. They're near-free relative to the films, they carry the platform between
flights, and they're what dealers will actually re-post.

## 6. The four conditions

**1. Apply the discipline filter ruthlessly.**
Every execution is a joke about the wasted asset, not about the cold (§4). This is what
keeps the work premium and keeps it un-copyable by cheaper competitors.

**2. Keep the craft at luxury standard.**
The joke lives in the *writing and the situation*. The film must look like an architecture
reel — cinema lensing, real houses, real light, no sitcom lighting, no zany sound effects,
no wacky music bed. The tone to brief a director on is **expensive deadpan**: Wes
Anderson's stillness, not a Super Bowl gag reel. This is the specific thing that protects
the specifier relationship, and if the client gets nervous about the whole idea, this is the
reassurance that unlocks it.

**3. Run a two-tier architecture.**
Do not aim one campaign at homeowners and architects simultaneously. *Get Bromic* is the
top-of-funnel fame layer. The trade needs a parallel lower-funnel **specification track** —
coverage charts, mounting and recess details, wind performance, marine grade, warranty,
CEU — that borrows the endline and the art direction but not the joke. Film 6 is the bridge
between them.

**4. Do not measure it on CTR.**
This is the way campaigns like this die: a dashboard shows a 0.4% CTR in week three,
someone panics, and the money gets moved to retargeting. Agree the measurement framework
*before* launch and get it signed (§9).

## 7. Audience and channel

### Tier 1 — Homeowners, 40–70, remodelling / adding on / building new

**Targeting.** Do *not* buy "patio heater" search intent as the primary — that's a small,
bottom-funnel, price-shopping pool, and it's where the category already competes. Buy the
moment *before* the category occurs to them:

- home renovation and custom-build intent; recently-moved, high-value homes
- luxury outdoor furniture, outdoor kitchen, pool and spa audiences
- premium appliance affinities (Sub-Zero, Wolf, Lynx, Gaggenau)
- Houzz / Architectural Digest / Dwell readership
- lookalikes built off dealer CRM and showroom lead lists — the highest-value seed you have

**Channels.** Meta (feed + Reels, 9:16 first), YouTube, and **CTV/streaming — which this
demo over-indexes on** and where a 30s film with production values actually gets watched.
Then Pinterest and Houzz for the statics.

**Geography.** Concentrate, don't spread. Real season + real money: Northeast, Mid-Atlantic,
Chicago, Denver/Mountain, Pacific Northwest, and coastal California (where the enemy is fog
and evening chill, not snow — that's a distinct, very large, very affluent pocket).

**Seasonality.** Consumer weight lands **August–November**; that window is the entire
consumer year. Renovation planning and spec activity runs **January–March** — that's the
trade tier's moment, not the homeowner's.

### Tier 2 — Architects, designers, custom builders, commercial

LinkedIn, trade press, ArchDaily, AIA-accredited CEU content, showroom and dealer co-op.
The funny films can run here — they'll be enjoyed — but the CTA is always the spec kit, the
Revit/CAD files, and the rep visit.

### The channel that matters most: the dealer

This category is closed at a counter, by a person. The campaign converts or dies in the
showroom. Budget for it explicitly:

- cut the films for dealers' own social accounts, co-branded
- in-store statics and window vinyls in the campaign's art direction
- a `Get Bromic` badge/loop for showroom display units
- a one-page sales enablement sheet built on the §2 reframe — *"the heater is 4% of the
  build and it's the difference between eleven months and two"*

## 8. Production

One 3-day shoot. One high-end modern property that can play three ways (pergola, outdoor
kitchen, pool terrace), plus a half-day for the "Layers" interior. One director. One
deadpan VO artist across the whole library. Cast of eight, reused.

**Output:** 6 × 30s, each cut to 15s and 6s, in three aspect ratios, plus ~20 statics
pulled from stills on the same shoot.

That is a full year's content library from one production, and it is the argument that
makes this affordable — mid-six figures for the whole thing, versus what the category
typically burns on a single golden-hour brand film that nobody remembers.

---

## 9. Measurement

**Primary (this is a fame campaign — measure fame):**
- **Branded search volume for "Bromic"** — the cleanest, fastest, cheapest read there is.
  Set the pre-flight baseline now.
- Unaided and aided brand awareness in the target, pre/post.
- **Dealer-reported "customer asked for Bromic by name"** — a single tracked field in the
  dealer portal. This is the metric that proves the whole thesis, and it's nearly free.

**Proof of commercial effect:**
- **Matched-market geo holdout.** Run in six DMAs, hold six comparable ones out, read
  dealer sell-through across both. This is the only credible causal ROI evidence for a
  brand play, and it's what protects the budget in year two.
- Platform brand-lift studies on Meta and YouTube.

**Diagnostics only, never optimisation targets:**
- 3s views, thruplay, completion rate, view-through site traffic.

**Explicitly not a KPI:** CTR, cost per click, cost per lead on the brand films. Write this
into the measurement doc and have the client sign it before a dollar is spent.

**Budget split:** roughly **60 brand / 40 activation** in year one. Do not starve the
always-on search and retargeting layer — the funny work manufactures demand, and something
has to be standing there to harvest it.

## 10. Risks, and how each one is handled

| Risk | Severity | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Attribution failure** — people remember the joke, forget the brand. The #1 killer of funny advertising. | High | Brand in-frame in the first 3s *and* the endline; product visible during the turn; a fixed audio mnemonic (the click + warm hum) on every cut including the 6s; one VO voice across the library. |
| **Alienating architects/specifiers**, who drive the volume | High | Luxury craft standard (§6.2); separate trade tier (§6.3); pre-show the work to 8–10 partner firms before launch and let them feel consulted. |
| **Commercial/hospitality tonal mismatch** — a restaurateur buying 40 units doesn't want jokes about being cold | Medium | Trade tier runs straight. Film 6 is the only humorous B2B asset, and its joke is about reputation, not discomfort. |
| **IP — *The Revenant*** | Medium | Never name it, never recreate a shot, never use the score, no visible animal harm. Legal-review Film 2 before production. Parody is defensible; imitation isn't. |
| **Down-market drift** — humour reading as cheap | Medium | The discipline filter (§4) plus craft standard. Every joke must cost money to tell. |
| **Competitive counter** — Infratech goes hard on price and Made-in-USA | Medium | Bake one product truth into each film's turn (gas capability, wind resistance, flush integration) so the humour still carries a reason to believe. Fame without a reason to believe loses to price. |
| **Humour doesn't travel AU↔US↔UK; seasons invert** | Low-Med | "Migration", "Wasteland" and "The Pergola" are culture-neutral — lead international with those. Flight AU six months offset. |
| **Client nerve fails in week 3** | High | §9, agreed and signed pre-launch. This is a real risk and it is managed with a document, not with optimism. |

## 11. What I'd change about the brief as written

- **Keep the name.** "Get Bromic" is genuinely good. Don't workshop it.
- **Keep "Wasteland" and make it the hero.** It's the strategic idea and the joke at the
  same time, which is rare.
- **Rebuild the horse, don't cut it.** Implication, not gore. Run it third or fourth.
- **Add the deadpan narrator** as connective tissue — it's what turns six films into one
  campaign and it costs almost nothing.
- **Add the filter** in §4. Without it, this drifts into generic cold-jokes inside two
  rounds and becomes a campaign any competitor could have run.
- **Add the trade tier** before presenting. The client's first objection will be "what will
  our architects think" — arrive with the answer already built.
