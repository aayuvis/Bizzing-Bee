Silent kn- / gn- / wr- (Old English)

The big idea
These initial consonant clusters come from Old English and Norse. The first consonant was once pronounced — you can still hear it in German (Knabe = boy, schreiben = to write). English pronunciation shifted; spelling froze.
The pro move
WRAITH
Say it. RAYTH Spell it. W · R · A · I · T · H Say it again. RAYTH Syllables. w · r · a · i · t · h Sounds → Letters. [r=WR] [ayth=AITH] Exception. wr- = /r/ only; w is completely silent; -ai- = /ay/; -th = /th/; Old English origin Origin. English Root. Scottish wraith = ghost, apparition (origin uncertain)

WR- (silent W)

wr- cluster from Old English: wraith (ghost), writhe (twist in pain), wrought (past of work), wrath (fury), wreak (inflict), wrest (twist away), wry (twisted/ironic), wring (twist tightly), wren (small bird). All start with a silent w — only the r is heard.

KN- (silent K)

Old Norse kn- cluster: knight, knave, kneel, knell (sound of a bell), knapsack, knack, knavish, knickers, knob, knoll (small hill), knot. The k was once pronounced — you can still hear it in German Knabe (boy) and Knie (knee).

GN- (silent G)

Gnome (small creature), gnarled (twisted), gnash (grind teeth), gnu (wildebeest), gnostic (gnosis = knowledge — the g was always silent in Greek), gnaw (chew persistently), gnarl (twisted lump). The g was once pronounced in Old English — you can still hear it in Italian gnocchi.

Why Spelling Froze

English pronunciation shifted dramatically during the Great Vowel Shift (1400–1700), but printing was already standardizing spelling before the shift was complete. Spelling froze at a historical pronunciation moment; pronunciation kept moving. This is why English spelling preserves sounds that are no longer spoken.

Bee Strategy

When you hear a word starting with /r/ (like "right" or "raith") that seems literary or archaic, ask for the origin. Old English → consider wr-. When you hear /n/ at the start of a formal or medieval word, ask: could it be kn-? These two patterns explain dozens of common spelling bee words.

Silent -gh- Patterns

The big idea
The -gh- cluster is Old English's most complex legacy. It was once pronounced as a guttural /kh/ sound (like German Bach). As that sound disappeared, the gh became silent — but stayed in the spelling, creating six different pronunciations for -ough alone.
The pro move
DISTRAUGHT
Say it. d-ih-s-t-r-AW-t Spell it. D · I · S · T · R · A · U · G · H · T Say it again. d-ih-s-t-r-AW-t Syllables. d · i · s · t · r · a · u · g · h · t Sounds → Letters. [dis=DIS] [TRAWT=TRAUGHT] Exception. -aught = /awt/ sound; gh is completely silent; from Old English trahtion = pulled; stress: dis-TRAWT Origin. English Root. Latin distractus = pulled apart, past participle of distrahere

-igh- = /eye/ sound

Night, knight, blight, flight, right, sight, tight, light, might, slight, fright, plight, bright. The -igh- sequence always = /eye/. The gh is completely silent. This is the most regular of the -gh- patterns.

-ough = 6 pronunciations (memorize as groups)

- /oh/: though, although, dough, furlough - /oo/: through, slough (a swamp) - /uf/: rough, tough, enough, slough (shed skin) - /of/: cough, trough - /aw/: thought, bought, fought, ought, nought, distraught, wrought - /up/: hiccough (= hiccup) Learn by pronunciation group, not individual words.

-aught = /awt/ sound

Distraught (pulled apart = extremely upset), onslaught (sudden violent attack), slaughter, naught (nothing), naughty, daughter, caught, taught, fraught (filled with anxiety). All use -aught = /awt/. This group is the most consistent.

-ight vs -ite

Night (Old English, -ight) vs site (Latin, -ite). Might (power) vs mite (a tiny creature). Right vs write vs rite — three spellings of one /rīt/ sound. When the /eye/ + /t/ sound appears in a word with Old English feel, the spelling is likely -ight.

Bee Strategy for -gh-

Ask for origin and definition. Old English origin + /awt/ sound → -aught. Old English + /eye/ sound → -ight. The /uf/ sound in a short word → likely -ough (rough, tough). The /oh/ sound in a word → could be -ough (though) or -ow (low) — definition distinguishes.

ie / ei Rule and Exceptions

The big idea
The classic rhyme: "i before e, except after c, or when sounding like /ay/ as in neighbor and weigh." This covers 80% of cases. The other 20% — the exceptions — are the ones that appear in spelling bees.
The pro move
SERENDIPITY
Say it. s-eh-r-uh-n-d-IH-p-ih-t-ee Spell it. S · E · R · E · N · D · I · P · I · T · Y Say it again. s-eh-r-uh-n-d-IH-p-ih-t-ee Syllables. s · e · r · e · n · d · i · p · i · t · y Sounds → Letters. [ser=SER] [en=EN] [DIP=DIP] [ih=I] [tee=TY] Exception. -i- before -p- here; no ie/ei issue in this word — but seize, weird, leisure all break the ie/ei rule Origin. French Root. Persian Serendip = old name for Sri Lanka

The Rule — i before e

After most consonants, ie = /ee/: believe, achieve, relieve, reprieve, grieve, retrieve, mischief, piece, niece. The mnemonic: "i before e" — the i comes first in most /ee/ sounds.

After c — e before i

After the letter c, the order reverses: ceiling, receive, perceive, deceive, conceive, deceit, conceit, receipt. The c flips the order. Mnemonic: the c is selfish — it pushes e in front of i.

When Sounding /ay/ — e before i

Vein, reign, rein, sleigh, weigh, beige, feign, feint, freight, neighbor, eight. When the vowel combination sounds like /ay/, e comes before i. The sound /ay/ overrides the normal order.

The Exceptions — Memorize These

Seize (not sieze), weird (not wierd), either (not iether), neither, leisure (not liesure), forfeit (not forfiet), foreign (not foriegn), height (not hieght), protein, caffeine, kaleidoscope, zeitgeist, species. These break the rule and must be memorized individually.

Why the Rule Has Exceptions

The rule was invented in 1866 to describe English spelling — English spelling was not invented to follow the rule. Words borrowed from German (zeitgeist, stein), French (beige, feign), and science (protein, caffeine) follow their source languages' spelling, not an English mnemonic invented centuries later.

Double Consonant Rules

The big idea
English doubles a final consonant before suffixes that begin with a vowel (-ing, -ed, -er, -est) under specific conditions. The rule is completely systematic — stress is the key.
The pro move
COMMITTED
Say it. kuh-MIH-tihd Spell it. C · O · M · M · I · T · T · E · D Say it again. kuh-MIH-tihd Syllables. kah · mih · tah · d Sounds → Letters. [kuh=COM] [MIT=MIT] [ed=TED] Exception. stress on -MIT so double T before -ed; commit + ted → committed; single-T would give /KOM-ih-ted/ Origin. Latin Root. Latin committere = to send or put together

The One-Syllable Rule (CVC)

Double the final consonant when a one-syllable word ends in consonant-vowel-consonant: run→running, stop→stopped, sit→sitting, cut→cutting, big→bigger. The doubling keeps the vowel short. Without it: runing would rhyme with tuning.

The Two-Syllable Stress Rule

For two-syllable words, double only if stress falls on the LAST syllable: begin→beginning (stress on BEGIN), occur→occurred (stress on OCCUR), commit→committed (stress on COMMIT), prefer→preferred (stress on preFER). Do NOT double if stress falls on the FIRST syllable: open→opening, happen→happened.

Test — Tap the Syllable

Tap your desk on each syllable and feel which one is louder. Travel: TRAV-el (first syllable louder) → traveled (no double). Control: con-TROL (second syllable louder) → controlled (double-l). Offer: OF-fer (first) → offered (no double). Occur: oc-CUR (second) → occurred (double-r).

American vs British Doubling

Americans: traveled, modeled, canceled. British: travelled, modelled, cancelled — they double whenever the final l is preceded by a single vowel, regardless of stress. For US spelling bees (Merriam-Webster): American conventions apply. Know which dictionary the bee uses.

Always-Doubled Words

Some words have inherited double consonants that are not about suffixes — they must be memorized: accommodate (double-c + double-m), embarrass (double-r + double-s), millennium (double-l + double-n), necessary (one-c + double-s), occasion (double-c + one-s), recommend (one-c + double-m), satellite (one-t + double-l).

c = /s/ Before e / i / y

The big idea
The letter c has two sounds: /k/ (hard) before a, o, u, and /s/ (soft) before e, i, y. Knowing this rule explains dozens of spelling patterns and helps decode words you've never seen.
The pro move
IRIDESCENT
Say it. ih-r-uh-d-EH-s-uh-n-t Spell it. I · R · I · D · E · S · C · E · N · T Say it again. ih-r-uh-d-EH-s-uh-n-t Syllables. i · r · i · d · e · s · c · e · n · t Sounds → Letters. [ir=IR] [ih=I] [DES=DES] [ent=CENT] Exception. -sc- = /s/ because c before e; -escent = becoming; -ir- not -irr-; stress: ir-ih-DES-ent Origin. English Root. Greek iris = rainbow + Latin -escent(becoming)

The Rule

c = /k/ before a, o, u, and consonants: cat, cold, cup, craft. c = /s/ before e, i, y: ceiling, city, cycle. This explains why cell starts with /s/ and call starts with /k/ — same letter, different vowel following it.

-sc- = /s/ Before e/i

The cluster -sc- before e or i also = /s/: iridescent (sc + e = /s/), adolescent, luminescent, quiescent, convalescent, effervescent, incandescent. The s in -sc- is redundant to the /s/ sound of c — but both letters must be spelled.

-ce and -ci- Inside Words

Susceptible (sus- + cep- + -ible) — the c before e → susceptible not susceptable. Conceit (con- + ceit) — c before e. Luscious — c before i. Precious — c before i. Spacious — c before i. Recognizing -ci- as /sh/ in suffixes like -cious, -cial, -cient is a major spelling pattern.

Acquiesce — the c/qu Combination

Acquiesce (ac- + quiescere = rest) — the qu keeps its /kw/ sound; the -esce has c before e = /s/. The word has two different c sounds: /kw/ (in qu) and /s/ (in -sc-). Spelling: A-C-Q-U-I-E-S-C-E.

Soft c vs Hard c in the Same Word

Circle = cir (soft c before i) + -cle (hard c before l). Circus = cir (soft c before i) + -cus (hard c before u). Concede = con (hard c) + -cede (soft c before e). The position of the vowel following the c tells you which sound it makes — every time.

ph = /f/ Sound (Greek Signal)

The big idea
ph = /f/ is the most reliable Greek origin signal in English. It appears ONLY in Greek-origin words (or words modeled on Greek). Hearing /f/ in a learned, scientific, or literary word = look for ph, not f.
The pro move
DIAPHANOUS
Say it. d-ay-A-f-uh-n-ih-s Spell it. D · I · A · P · H · A · N · O · U · S Say it again. d-ay-A-f-uh-n-ih-s Syllables. d · i · a · p · h · a · n · o · u · s Sounds → Letters. [die=DI] [AF=APH] [uh=A] [nus=NOUS] Exception. -ph- = /f/ (Greek!); dia- = through; phanein = show; -ous suffix; die-AF-uh-nus Origin. English Root. Greek diaphainein = to show through

The Golden Rule

If you hear /f/ in a learned, scientific, or literary word — write ph not f. Phenomenon, ephemeral, diphtheria, phosphorus, sycophant, sophistry, metaphor, diaphanous, asphyxia, phlegm. The ph is Greek phi (φ), and it entered English with the Greek vocabulary.

The Exception

Common everyday English words use f not ph: fun, fish, finger, fly, fog, flat. The rule only applies to borrowed learned words. Fahrenheit = German, uses f. Fan = Old English, uses f. If it is a short, everyday word you knew as a child → probably f. If it is a learned word you encountered in books → probably ph.

ph in the Middle of Words

Elephant (elephas = Greek) — ph in the middle. Orphan (orphanos = Greek) — ph in the middle. Emphasis (emphasis = Greek) — ph in the middle. Amphitheater — ph inside. Epiphany — ph inside. Morphine — ph inside. The ph appears anywhere in the word, not just at the start.

The Hardest ph Clusters

Diphthong = di- + phthongos = two sounds merged — has -phth- cluster (two silent-seeming letters working together). Ophthalmology = -phth- cluster inside. Phthisis (tuberculosis) starts with pht- — P-H-T-H-I-S-I-S. These are the most extreme Greek ph spellings.

Building ph Word Families

-phage (eating): sarcophagus (flesh eater). -phor (carrying): metaphor, phosphor. -phyte (plant): lithophyte, epiphyte. -phon (sound): microphone, saxophone. -phil (loving): bibliophile, anglophile. One pattern — the ph — unlocks them all.

qu- Combination (q Always Followed by u)

The big idea
In English, the letter q is almost always followed by u. The combination qu represents two different sounds: /kw/ in most English words, and /k/ in French-origin words ending in -que.
The pro move
ELOQUENT
Say it. EH-luh-kwuhnt Spell it. E · L · O · Q · U · E · N · T Say it again. EH-luh-kwuhnt Syllables. eh · lah · kwah · nt Sounds → Letters. [EL=EL] [uh=O] [kwent=QUENT] Exception. -oqu- = /uh-kw/; eloqui = speak out; -ent suffix; stress: EL-uh-kwent Origin. Latin Root. Latin eloqui = to speak out

The Rule

In English, q is almost always followed by u. The qu cluster represents /kw/ in most words: queen, quick, question, quiet, quorum, quandary, quarrel, quarrel, querulous, quorum. There is almost never a q without a u in a standard English word.

qu = /k/ (French-origin words)

In French-origin words ending in -que, the qu = /k/ not /kw/: bouquet (boo-KAY), clique (kleek), opaque (oh-PAYK), unique (yoo-NEEK), oblique (oh-BLEEK), technique (tek-NEEK), antique (an-TEEK). The -que ending signals French origin and /k/ sound.

qu Inside Latin Words

Eloquent (e- + loqui = speak) — the qu = /kw/. Soliloquy — qu = /kw/. Colloquy — qu = /kw/. Ventriloquist — qu = /kw/. In Latin-derived words, qu almost always = /kw/. In French-derived words ending in -que, it = /k*.

Exceptions — q Without u

Rare borrowed words have q without u: qi (Chinese life-force), qigong (Chinese breathing practice), qanat (Persian underground waterway), qoph (Hebrew letter). These come from languages where q represents a different sound than in Latin/Greek/French. At a bee: ask for origin.

Soliloquy vs Colloquy

Soliloquy = S-O-L-I-L-O-Q-U-Y (speaking alone — the qu before y). Colloquy = C-O-L-L-O-Q-U-Y. Ventriloquist = V-E-N-T-R-I-L-O-Q-U-I-S-T. In all three, the qu is followed by a vowel (y or u or i). The q always needs its u — and then another vowel follows to complete the syllable.

y as Vowel (Greek Words)

The big idea
In English words of Greek origin, y functions as a vowel, sounding like /i/ or /ee/. This is Greek letter upsilon (Υ), which became y when Greek words entered Latin and then English.
The pro move
LABYRINTH
Say it. l-A-b-ur-ih-n-th Spell it. L · A · B · Y · R · I · N · T · H Say it again. l-A-b-ur-ih-n-th Syllables. l · a · b · y · r · i · n · t · h Sounds → Letters. [LAB=LAB] [uh=Y] [rinth=RINTH] Exception. y = /uh/ here (as vowel, not consonant); -th = /th/ from Greek theta; Greek origin; LAB-uh-rinth Origin. English Root. Greek labyrinthos = maze, possibly from labrys = double-headed axe

The Pattern

When y appears in the MIDDLE of a word (not at the end), it is usually Greek and functions as a vowel, sounding like /i/ or /uh/: rhythm, crypt, lymph, nymph, gymnasium, typhoon, oxygen, labyrinth, syllable, symbol, cyst, gypsum, myth, myrtle.

Y Clusters — Middle of Word

-yn-: synonym, syntax, synthesis, synapse. -ym-: symbol, symmetry, sympathy, symphony. -yr-: pyramid, lyric, lyre, myrtle, tyranny, myrrh. -ys-: system, mystery, crystalline. -yp-: typical, type, typhoon, hypnosis.

Y at the End = /ee/

When y ends a word, it sounds like /ee/ in Greek-derived words: harmony, melody, euphony, biology, geography. This is the -y from Greek -ia or -eia. Different sound, same Greek origin. End = /ee/. Middle = /i/ or /uh/.

Medical Y Words

Psych- words: psychology, psychiatry, psyche — the p is silent AND y is a vowel. Gymn- words: gymnasium, gymnast, gymnastics — y is a vowel. Crypt- words: cryptic, cryptogram — y is a vowel. Cyst, lymph, glyph — all single-syllable medical words with y as the only vowel.

Why y?* Greek letter upsilon (Υ, υ) was a vowel in Greek. When Romans transcribed Greek words, they wrote y for upsilon (because they had no upsilon in Latin). English inherited this Roman convention. So every English word with a vowel-sounding y in the middle is a direct descendant of a Greek upsilon*. **Word Bank

-ough: Six Pronunciations

The big idea
The most notoriously irregular spelling pattern in English: -ough represents six distinct sounds with no sound-based rule for which applies. Memorizing the words by sound group is the only reliable strategy.
The pro move
THOROUGH
Say it. THUR-oh Spell it. T · H · O · R · O · U · G · H Say it again. THUR-oh Syllables. ther · ow Sounds → Letters. [THUR=THOR] [oh=OUGH] Exception. -ough = /oh/ here (the thorough/borough group); -th = /th/; silent gh; stress: THUR-oh Origin. Old English Root. Old English þuruh = through, complete

Group 1 — /oh/ (rhymes with "go")

Though (conjunction), although, dough (bread dough), furlough (temporary leave), thorough (complete — also pronounced /thur-oh/). Mnemonic: though the dough rises, the soldier on furlough is thorough.

Group 2 — /oo/ and /uf/

/oo/ (rhymes with "true"): through (from one side to the other), slough (a swamp in American English). /uf/ (rhymes with "stuff"): rough, tough, enough, slough (to shed skin). ⚠️ Slough has TWO pronunciations with TWO meanings — /sloo/ = swamp, /sluf/ = shed skin.

Group 3 — /aw/ and /of/

/aw/ (rhymes with "law"): thought, bought, fought, ought, nought (zero), wrought (shaped by work), distraught (extremely upset), onslaught. /of/ (rhymes with "off"): cough, trough (a feeding container). The /aw/ group is the largest.

Group 4 — /up/ and Outliers

/up/: hiccough = an old spelling of hiccup — the -ough is historically irregular even here. Borough = /BUR-oh/. Thorough = /THUR-oh/. These end in -ough but sound like -oh. They share the /oh/ group pronunciation but are worth noting separately because of spelling-pronunciation mismatch.

Bee Strategy for -ough Words

When you hear a word and suspect -ough, ask for the definition and origin. Old English origin + /aw/ sound → almost certainly -ough (thought, bought). /uf/ sound in a short word → -ough (rough, tough). /oh/ sound → could be -ough (though) or -ow (low). Definition distinguishes. Spell the gh — it is always there, always silent.

Homophones (Same Sound, Different Spelling)

The big idea
Homophones are words that sound identical but are spelled differently and mean different things. They cannot be decoded from sound alone — etymology and context are the only guides.
The pro move
PRINCIPLE
Say it. PRIH-nsuh-puhl Spell it. P · R · I · N · C · I · P · L · E Say it again. PRIH-nsuh-puhl Syllables. prih · nsah · pah · l Sounds → Letters. [PRIN=PRIN] [suh=CI] [pul=PLE] Exception. principal vs principle: both /PRIN-suh-pul/; -al = adjective/noun (a person); -le = abstract (a rule) Origin. French Root. Latin principium = beginning, foundation

The Core Pairs

Principal (the head of a school, or primary) vs Principle (a fundamental rule). Memory trick: the principal is your pal; a principle is a learned rule. Complement (complete something) vs Compliment (praise). Stationary (not moving) vs Stationery (paper products — the er in stationery = paper).

More Critical Pairs

Affect (verb = to influence) vs Effect (noun = the result). Accept (to receive) vs Except (excluding). Elicit (to draw out) vs Illicit (not permitted). Eminent (famous, prominent) vs Imminent (about to happen). Allude (hint at) vs Elude (escape from).

Triple Homophones

Their (belonging to them) / There (a place) / They're (they are). To (preposition) / Two (the number) / Too (also/excessively). Bare (uncovered) / Bear (the animal, or to carry). Three spellings, one sound — context is everything.

Etymology Resolves Homophones

Stationary comes from Latin stationarius = standing still. Stationery comes from stationer = a bookshop owner who had a fixed station (shop). The -er at the end of stationery comes from the stationer who sold paper. Knowing the origin makes the spelling logical.

Disinterested vs Uninterested

Disinterested = having no personal stake = impartial (a dis- = away from interest judge). Uninterested = not interested = bored (un- = simply not). These are not homophones — they sound different and mean different things — but they are the most commonly confused pair in educated writing.

Contronyms (Same Word, Opposite Meanings)

The big idea
Contronyms (also called autoantonyms) are words with two opposite meanings. Context determines which meaning applies. English has dozens of these from centuries of word-meaning drift.
The pro move
SANCTION
Say it. SA-ngkshuhn Spell it. S · A · N · C · T · I · O · N Say it again. SA-ngkshuhn Syllables. sae · ngkshah · n Sounds → Letters. [SANK=SANC] [shun=TION] Exception. Latin sancire = to make sacred; sanction = to approve OR to penalize — opposite meanings; stress: SANK-shun Origin. French Root. Latin sanctio = a decree making something sacred (sanctus=holy)

Classic Contronyms

Sanction = to officially approve OR to penalize with restrictions. Cleave = to split apart OR to stick closely together. Oversight = careful supervision OR a failure to notice. Buckle = to fasten firmly OR to bend and collapse. Dust = to add dust (dusting crops) OR to remove dust (dusting furniture).

More Contronyms

Screen = to show (screen a film) OR to hide (screen from view). Weather = to withstand (weather a storm) OR to erode (rock weathers over time). Trip = to stumble and fall OR to take a journey. Off = active (the alarm went off) OR inactive (the alarm is off). Fast = moving quickly OR stuck firmly in place.

Contronyms in Legal Language

Enjoin = to order someone to do something OR to prohibit someone from doing something. Cleave appears in wedding vows: "cleave unto" = hold closely together. In cooking: "cleave the meat" = split apart. The same word, used centuries apart, accumulated both meanings.

Why Contronyms Exist

Some developed from different etymological paths meeting in the same spelling. Sanction comes from Latin sanctio = a decree — a decree can authorize or prohibit. Cleave has TWO Old English roots: cleofan (to split) and clifian (to stick). They merged in spelling but kept opposite meanings.

Contronyms in Spelling Bees (Vocabulary Rounds)

Inflammable = can catch fire (NOT fireproof) — in- here means "into flames" not "not." Invaluable = so valuable it cannot be valued = priceless (NOT worthless). These are the most dangerous contronyms because the in- prefix appears to negate the meaning but does not.

Eponyms (Words from People's Names)

The big idea
Eponyms are words derived from people's names — real or legendary. English has hundreds of them, from boycott to algorithm. Knowing the story behind an eponym makes the spelling unforgettable.
The pro move
BOYCOTT
Say it. BOY-kaht Spell it. B · O · Y · C · O · T · T Say it again. BOY-kaht Syllables. boy · cott Sounds → Letters. [BOY=BOY] [kot=COTT] Exception. From Captain Charles Boycott (Irish land agent, 1880); double-T not single; stress: BOY-kot Origin. Other Root. Named after Captain Charles Boycott (1832–1897), a land agent in Irela

Classic Eponyms

Boycott = from Captain Charles Boycott, an Irish land agent who was socially ostracized. Sandwich = from the Earl of Sandwich who ate bread around his food so he could keep gambling. Cardigan = from Lord Cardigan who wore a knitted jacket at the Battle of Balaclava. Wellington (boot) = from the Duke of Wellington.

Scientific Eponyms

Watt (unit of power) = from James Watt, inventor. Fahrenheit = from Daniel Fahrenheit. Celsius = from Anders Celsius. Pasteurize = from Louis Pasteur. Galvanize = from Luigi Galvani (electricity). Algorithm = from Al-Khwarizmi, the Arab mathematician. Jacuzzi = from Candido Jacuzzi.

Literary Eponyms

Malapropism = from Mrs. Malaprop in Sheridan's play The Rivals (1775). Quixotic = from Don Quixote, Cervantes' idealistic knight. Odyssey = from Odysseus (Ulysses) and his long journey. Mentor = from Mentor, Odysseus's trusted advisor. Panic = from Pan, the Greek god who caused sudden irrational terror.

Words from Mythology as Eponyms

Narcissism = from Narcissus. Tantalize = from Tantalus (in the underworld, food and water always out of reach). Herculean = from Hercules. Jovial = from Jove/Jupiter (those born under Jupiter were thought cheerful). Mercurial = from Mercury (quick-moving, unpredictable).

Spelling Eponyms

They generally keep the capitalized person's spelling, then lowercased: Boycott = two t's because the name Boycott has two t's. Cardigan = no e before -an because the name is Cardigan. Machiavellian = M-A-C-H-I-A-V-E-L-L-I-A-N (double-l from Machiavelli's name). Quixotic = Q-U-I-X-O-T-I-C (x from Spanish Quixote).
